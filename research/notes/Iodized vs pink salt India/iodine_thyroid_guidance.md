## Q1. What does iodine do for the thyroid, what does deficiency do to adults, and why does it matter in pregnancy and breastfeeding?

### Takeaway
Iodine is a structural part of the thyroid hormones T4 and T3, so the thyroid cannot make hormone without it. Needs go up in pregnancy and breastfeeding (WHO: 250 µg/day, against 150 µg for other adults), because deficiency in that window harms fetal brain development. Indian survey data show that women of reproductive age are currently, on average, in the adequate range.

### Cited Findings
- "Iodine is an essential component of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)." Publisher: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Iodine Fact Sheet for Health Professionals. Date not confirmed (the page has a change history). Snippet only. [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)
- The Daily Value (DV) for iodine on US food labels is 150 mcg for adults and children aged 4 and over. A DV is a labelling value, not an RDA. [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)
- The adult Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is 1,100 mcg/day. NIH ODS notes that ULs do not apply to people taking iodine for medical reasons under a doctor's care. Consumer fact sheet, snippet. [NIH ODS consumer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-Consumer/)
- A 2007 WHO/UNICEF technical consultation proposed raising the FAO/WHO recommended nutrient intake for pregnancy from 200 to 250 µg/day, with 250 µg also applying to lactation. Publisher: Public Health Nutrition, 2007 (Andersson et al., WHO Secretariat). [PubMed 18053281](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18053281/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980007361004)
- WHO eLENA gives the pregnancy and lactation requirement as 250 µg/day. WHO and UNICEF recommend iodine supplementation in pregnancy and lactation only in certain settings, for example where fewer than 50% of households have iodized salt. [WHO eLENA: iodine in pregnancy](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/commentary/iodine-pregnancy)
- Requirements rise in pregnancy because thyroid hormone production increases, the kidneys excrete more iodine, and the fetus needs iodine. Severe deficiency can cause maternal and fetal hypothyroidism. Same eLENA/consultation material, snippet. [WHO eLENA](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/commentary/iodine-pregnancy)
- A meta-analysis cited in the WHO-linked literature estimated that iodine deficiency in a population lowered mean IQ by 13.5 points. This is a population-level estimate from older, mostly severe-deficiency data. Snippet seen in results related to the 2007 consultation. The primary meta-analysis was not located. [Public Health Nutrition 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980007361004)
- ATA (2013 statement, republished in *Thyroid* 2015) recommends that women take a multivitamin with 150 mcg iodine daily as potassium iodide during preconception, pregnancy and lactation. That advice is aimed at the US, where salt iodization is not universal. [ATA statement](https://www.thyroid.org/ata-statement-on-the-potential-risks-of-excess-iodine-ingestion-and-exposure); [Thyroid 2015, PubMed 25275241](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275241/)
- **India status:** India Iodine Survey 2018-19 (Nutrition International with ICCIDD; fieldwork by Kantar; 21,406 households in all states and UTs). National median urinary iodine: 173.4 µg/L in pregnant women, 172.8 µg/L in lactating women, 178.0 µg/L in non-pregnant, non-lactating women. Authors describe these as "within the adequate iodine nutrition range". 76.3% of households used adequately iodized salt (≥15 ppm). Coverage was lowest in Tamil Nadu and highest in Jammu and Kashmir. Report released September 2019; peer-reviewed analysis in *Journal of Nutrition*, 2023. [Nutrition International report PDF](https://www.nutritionintl.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/India-Iodine-Survey-2018-19-final-report.pdf); [J Nutr 2023 record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-37390906)
- NFHS household use of iodized salt was 93.1% in NFHS-4 (2015-16). Down To Earth's analysis of NFHS-5 (2019-21) state data reports high coverage in most states, with Andhra Pradesh and Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu among the lowest. I did not find the national NFHS-5 figure. [Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/health/what-does-nfhs-5-tell-us-about-india-s-iodine-consumption-74973)

### Inferences
- Indian women in their 30s to 50s who already cook with iodized salt are, as a group, probably getting enough iodine. The case for iodized salt is to keep that up, not to fix a widespread deficiency. Switching to non-iodized salts such as pink, rock or sendha removes this dependable source.
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding are when iodine adequacy matters most. Many readers aged 30 to 40 may be planning a pregnancy.

### Gaps
- I could not check the NIH ODS RDA table (adult 150 mcg, pregnancy 220 mcg, lactation 290 mcg) on the page. The search snippet cut off before those rows. These are the standard US DRI values but are unconfirmed here.
- I could not confirm NIH ODS wording on adult deficiency effects (goitre, hypothyroidism). The search snippets did not cover it. Standard knowledge, but no verified quote.
- I could not confirm the iodine content of iodized salt per teaspoon (the NIH ODS US figure is often cited as about 45 mcg per 1/8 tsp). India's standard is ≥15 ppm at consumer level, per the India Iodine Survey. I did not find the FSSAI/NIDDCP figures (30 ppm at production, 15 ppm at consumer).

## Q2. Do WHO and ICMR say salt reduction and salt iodization are compatible? Do Indians eat too much salt?

### Takeaway
Yes, they are compatible. WHO recommends adults eat under 5 g of salt a day (under 2 g sodium) and says all salt eaten should be iodized. Its guidance states that the two policies work together, with iodization levels adjusted as intake falls. ICMR-NIN 2024 likewise says to use iodized salt and keep added salt to 5 g/day at most. Indians eat about 8 g/day on average (ICMR-NCDIR).

### Cited Findings
- WHO recommends adults consume less than 2,000 mg sodium a day, equal to less than 5 g salt (just under a teaspoon). For children aged 2 to 15, the amount is adjusted downward for energy needs. [WHO eLENA: reducing sodium intake in adults](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/sodium-cvd-adults); [WHO fact sheet: Salt reduction](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction)
- WHO's salt reduction fact sheet says all salt that is consumed should be iodized (fortified with iodine), which is "essential for healthy brain development in the foetus and young child". Snippet; fact sheet date not confirmed. [WHO fact sheet: Salt reduction](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction)
- WHO's sodium guideline states that salt reduction and salt iodization are compatible, and says countries should monitor salt intake and iodization so that iodization can be adjusted over time as needed. [WHO eLENA](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/sodium-cvd-adults)
- WHO's 2014 guideline on fortifying food-grade salt with iodine for preventing and controlling iodine deficiency disorders recommends fortifying all food-grade salt used in households and food processing. The recommendation wording was not read on the page. [WHO 2014 guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241507929)
- A 2022 WHO brief says universal salt iodization and sodium reduction policies are "compatible and cost-effective" and urges the two programme communities to work together. [WHO publication 9789240053717](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240053717)
- PAHO/WHO 2011 white paper: cutting salt to under 5 g/day is compatible with a mean salt iodine level of about 40 ppm. This is a regional technical paper, not a global guideline. [PAHO white paper 2011](https://paho.org/sites/default/files/White-paper-optimum-intake-sodium-iodine-2011-Eng.pdf)
- WHO now also recommends potassium-enriched, lower-sodium salt substitutes for adults (2025 guideline), and a WHO-linked summary says these should also be iodized. Secondary summary by Resolve to Save Lives. [RTSL overview of WHO LSS guideline](https://resolvetosavelives.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/RTSL_WHO-LSS-Guidelines-Overview.pdf)
- ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024 (released May 2024): "Use iodized salt"; restrict added salt (sodium chloride) to a maximum of 5 g/day. They link high salt intake to high blood pressure, heart and vascular disease, and stomach cancer risk. They also say "irrespective of the variety of salt, their consumption should be limited as the sodium content in them is almost similar". These quotes come from secondary reports; the NIN PDF was not opened. [ICMR-NIN DGI 2024 PDF](https://www.nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI24thJune2024fin.pdf); [All India Radio News](https://newsonair.gov.in/icmr-releases-upgraded-dietary-guidelines-for-indians); [Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/mdtv/diet-nutrition/videos/icmr-releases-new-salt-intake-guidelines-after-discovering-similar-sodium-levels-in-all-salts-129964)
- **Indian salt intake:** ICMR-NCDIR National NCD Monitoring Survey 2017-18 (Mathur et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2023). Estimated mean salt intake is 8.0 g/day (men 8.9 g, women 7.1 g), above WHO's limit of 5 g. Estimates came from spot urine samples, not the gold-standard 24-hour collection. Higher intake was found in men, employed people, tobacco users, people with obesity and people with raised blood pressure. Awareness of harm and practices to limit salt were low. The analysed sample size is reported inconsistently (a sub-sample of about 3,000 versus 10,659 participants overall). [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-42694-x); [PMC10517942](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10517942/)

### Inferences
- The post can safely say: "Eat less salt overall, and make the salt you do eat iodized." WHO and ICMR-NIN both say this.
- ICMR's "sodium content is almost similar across varieties" line answers the idea that pink or rock salt is a healthier, lower-sodium choice. Swapping to them gives no sodium benefit and can lose iodine.
- Even at WHO's 5 g/day, iodized salt is designed to supply enough iodine, because iodization levels are set with salt reduction in mind.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm whether the ICMR-NIN 2024 document gives its own figure for current Indian salt intake. One news report said 8 to 11 g/day, but that is unverified and the source is a weak secondary one.
- I could not read the exact recommendation wording of the WHO 2014 iodization guideline.

## Q3. Excess iodine, Hashimoto's and supplements: what do ATA, NIH ODS and reviews say? Is iodized salt at normal intake a problem? Is "don't take iodine supplements without a doctor" supported?

### Takeaway
Authoritative sources warn about high-dose iodine supplements, kelp and seaweed pills, not iodized salt. ATA advises against iodine or kelp supplements above 500 mcg/day and names people with pre-existing thyroid disease, including Hashimoto's, as especially susceptible to excess iodine. Reviews link excess population iodine to more thyroid autoimmunity, especially where iodization programmes are poorly monitored. None of the sources found advises people with Hashimoto's to avoid iodized salt in normal amounts.

### Cited Findings
- ATA statement on the potential risks of excess iodine ingestion and exposure (2013; published in *Thyroid* 2015, Leung et al.). It advises against ingesting iodine and kelp supplements containing more than 500 mcg iodine daily, for children and adults and during pregnancy and lactation. It also says "ingestion of greater than 1,100 mcg of iodine per day (Tolerable Upper Limit for iodine) is not recommended and may cause thyroid dysfunction". Wording taken from search snippets of the ATA page and the *Thyroid* paper. [ATA statement](https://www.thyroid.org/ata-statement-on-the-potential-risks-of-excess-iodine-ingestion-and-exposure); [Thyroid 2015, PubMed 25275241](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275241/); [full text, U-Michigan Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/140261/thy.2014.0331.pdf?sequence=1)
- The same ATA paper lists people with pre-existing thyroid disease (for example autoimmune Hashimoto's disease, Graves' disease, nontoxic nodular goitre) as a group susceptible to the adverse effects of excess iodine. It also states there is "no known thyroid benefit of routine daily iodine doses in excess of the U.S. RDA". [Thyroid 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275241/); [ATA statement](https://www.thyroid.org/ata-statement-on-the-potential-risks-of-excess-iodine-ingestion-and-exposure)
- NIH ODS consumer fact sheet: high iodine intake can cause some of the same symptoms as deficiency, including goitre, and also thyroid gland inflammation, with possible thyroid cancer risk. Adult UL is 1,100 mcg/day. Snippet. [NIH ODS consumer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-Consumer/)
- Luo Y et al. (2014), "Iodine excess as an environmental risk factor for autoimmune thyroid disease", *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*. The review describes excess iodine as a precipitating environmental factor for thyroid autoimmunity, says iodine excess is now more common than deficiency in some regions, and notes high-iodine drinking water in parts of China as a source. [PubMed 25050783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25050783/); [PMC4139880](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4139880/)
- Vargas-Uricoechea H et al. (2025), "Iodine Intake from Universal Salt Iodization Programs and Hashimoto's Thyroiditis: A Systematic Review", *Diseases* 13(6):166, published 23 May 2025. The review covered literature from 1965 to January 2025, found 74 studies, and 31 of them assessed thyroid antibodies after USI. The authors conclude that excess iodine from USI programmes "without an adequate follow-up and monitoring plan" may explain, at least in part, the prevalence and distribution of Hashimoto's. They acknowledge that USI has greatly reduced iodine deficiency disorders. Abstract-level only. [PubMed 40558577](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40558577/); [MDPI full text](https://mdpi.com/2079-9721/13/6/166)
- A 2022 review (PMC8106604) argues that the relationship between population iodine intake and autoimmune thyroid disease is more complex than for non-autoimmune thyroid disorders. Title and authors not captured in the snippet. [PMC8106604](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8106604)
- An older review (1992) reported that autoimmune thyroid disease increased after iodine prophylaxis (iodized salt, bread or oil) in goitre-endemic areas. [PubMed 1345585](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1345585/)

### Evidence strength
- **Strong / consensus:** iodine is required for T4 and T3; deficiency in pregnancy harms fetal neurodevelopment; WHO and ICMR say salt reduction plus iodized salt is the recommended approach; Indian mean salt intake is about 8 g/day (one large national survey, spot-urine method).
- **Moderate (expert consensus plus observational data):** high-dose iodine supplements and kelp can cause thyroid dysfunction; people with Hashimoto's are more susceptible (ATA position, observational and population studies).
- **Moderate to weak, contested:** population-level iodization raises Hashimoto's or antibody prevalence. This is mostly observational, confounded by better detection and testing, and the effect is concentrated where intake overshoots adequacy. The 2025 systematic review puts the problem on unmonitored programmes, not on adequate intake.
- **Not found:** any guideline telling people with Hashimoto's to avoid iodized salt in normal cooking amounts.

### Inferences
- "Don't take iodine supplements or kelp/seaweed pills without your doctor's advice, especially with Hashimoto's" is well supported by the ATA statement (>500 mcg/day caution, Hashimoto's named as susceptible, no benefit above the RDA).
- "Iodized salt in normal cooking amounts is fine" is consistent with WHO, ICMR-NIN and the Indian survey data, which show women's median urinary iodine in the adequate range and not excessive. No authoritative source found tells Hashimoto's patients to drop iodized salt. Phrase it as "official guidance recommends iodized salt for everyone", not as "proven safe for Hashimoto's".
- Keeping salt to 5 g/day or less also limits total iodine from salt. The two messages support each other.
- Under the repo's health rules: do not give iodine doses, and do not suggest changing thyroid medication (levothyroxine).

- I could not read the exact NIH ODS health-professional section on autoimmune thyroid disease. The snippets did not reach it.
- I found no India-specific study on iodized salt and Hashimoto's incidence.
- The ATA statement dates from 2013/2015. I found no newer ATA update, but did not verify that none exists.
