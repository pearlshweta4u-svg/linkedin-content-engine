## Q1. What do peer-reviewed analyses find about iodine content of pink/Himalayan/rock/sea/black salt vs iodized salt?

### Takeaway
Every analysis I found that measured them puts non-iodized "natural" salts (Himalayan pink, rock/sendha, black/kala namak, plain sea salt) at negligible to undetectable iodine, roughly below 2-5 mg/kg. Iodized salt is fortified to tens of mg/kg (India: at least 15 ppm at retail). Evidence strength: **well established** that specialty salts are not reliable iodine sources. Peer-reviewed numbers specific to India are **limited** (two small market studies, read only as abstracts).

### Cited Findings
- **India, Delhi (2026):** Zakir Husain Delhi College (University of Delhi), Dept. of Chemistry. "From Salt to Health: Iodine Content in Market Available Salts and its Significance", *Current Research in Nutrition and Food Science*, Vol 14, special issue "Sustainable Nutrition" (2026). Abstract: "non-iodised salt varieties, including black salt, rock salt, and pink salt, demonstrated negligible to undetectable iodine levels." The authors also say rock-salt minerals are "present in very small amounts and a very high intake of the rock salt would be required" to matter nutritionally, which would mean excess sodium. They also discuss how iodine is lost from iodized salt in storage. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY: sample size and ppm values not seen] — [Current Research in Nutrition and Food Science](https://www.foodandnutritionjournal.org/vol14nospl-issue-sustainable-nutrition-2026/from-salt-to-health-iodine-content-in-market-available-salts-and-its-significance/)
- **India, Mohali, Punjab (2023):** *Food Chemistry Advances* (Elsevier), Dec 2023, Central University of Punjab authors. 14 coloured edible salts bought from Mohali shops. "the recommended level of iodine (I) fortification (≥50% RDA) was found only in 20% of the salts studied." [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY: exact values and the per-salt breakdown not seen] — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/640c0728b0b044998aeb90b8a0f2922c); [CUP repository](https://kr.cup.edu.in/handle/32116/4384)
- **New Zealand (2024):** *New Zealand Medical Journal*, Vol 137 No 1597 (2024), "Does iodised salt sold in New Zealand contain enough iodine?". Non-iodised salts tested, pink Himalayan salt among them, had a "median of <2mg iodine/kg salt, with a range of <1–3.5mg iodine/kg salt." [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [NZMJ PDF](https://nzmj.org.nz/media/pages/journal/vol-137-no-1597/does-iodised-salt-sold-in-new-zealand-contain-enough-iodine/69bd3daa63-1718768420/6475.pdf)
- **Slovenia (2016):** Žmitek K, Pravst I. "Iodisation of Salt in Slovenia: Increased Availability of Non-Iodised Salt in the Food Supply." *Nutrients* 2016;8(7):434. 86 salt products sampled in 2014. Non-iodised salts (the category includes gourmet sea salt and Himalayan salt) had a sales-weighted average of 3.5 mg KI/kg (range 1.3–5.2). Iodised salts averaged 24.2 mg KI/kg (range 20.9–45.8). Iodised sea salt and iodised rock salt (about 24 mg KI/kg each) show that "sea" or "rock" origin is not what matters; added iodine is. Non-iodised salt made up 33.7% of products but only 4.1% of sales. [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC4963910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4963910/)
- **Switzerland, Federal Food Safety and Veterinary Office (FSVO), 2016:** a study of 25 salts. Iodine in fortified salts was the main nutrient exception, and specialty salts "do not possess any advantages over standard salts." The FSVO said the speciality salts examined were "lacking in iodine". [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY; report hosted on a Warwick University page, summary on Alimentarium] — [FSVO report PDF (Warwick host)](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/med/staff/cappuccio/who/report_composition-of-prelevant-salt-varieties_2016.pdf); [Alimentarium](https://www.alimentarium.org/en/story/exotic-salt)
- **NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (US), Iodine Health Professional Fact Sheet:** "specialty salts, such as sea salt, kosher salt, Himalayan salt, and fleur de sel, are not usually iodized." Its food table lists non-iodized sea salt at 0 mcg per ¼ teaspoon. Measured US iodized salt averaged 47.5–50.7 mcg iodine/g. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY; page could not be opened, update date not confirmed] — [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)
- **Australia (2020), pink salt minerals:** Fayet-Moore F et al. "An Analysis of the Mineral Composition of Pink Salt Available in Australia." *Foods* 2020;9(10):1490 (published 19 Oct 2020), doi:10.3390/foods9101490. 31 pink salt samples, mostly Himalayan. The 25 minerals analysed did NOT include iodine, so this paper cannot be cited for an iodine number. [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC7603209](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603209)
- **India, fluoride in salts (2024):** Pai A et al., *Cureus* 2024;16(8):e66418 (8 Aug 2024). Compared Indian iodized (Tata Salt Superlite, Keerthi Nirmal), Himalayan pink, rock and black salt brands for fluoride only. No iodine measured. Mean fluoride: black salt 77.5 ppm, rock 40.8, pink 20.0, iodized 12.5. The authors call all of these "well within the prescribed limits". Useful only to show that the "mineral" differences between these salts are about other elements, not iodine. [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC11380087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11380087)
- **Iodized salt can lose iodine:** Krishna E et al., *J Family Med Prim Care* 2022;11(3):847–851. Review of Indian data. Salt kept in its intact original pack retained ~98.8% of iodine at 1 month and 91.7% at 3 months. In a glass jar: 94.3% and 69.4%. Cooking losses cited: boiling ~40%, microwave ~27%, roasting ~10.6%, deep frying ~10.4%. Advice: don't buy salt more than 6 months past manufacture, store it in the original packet. [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC9051721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9051721/)
- **US iodized salt also varies:** Dasgupta PK et al., *Environ Sci Technol* 2008 (PubMed 18351111). 47 of 88 US iodized salt samples were below the US FDA recommended iodine level and 6 were above it. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [PubMed 18351111](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18351111/)

### Inferences
- Whether a salt is "pink", "rock", "sea" or "black" does not decide its iodine content. Whether iodine was added does. Iodised rock salt and iodised sea salt exist (Slovenia data; India's BIS IS 7224 covers iodized salt made from rock salt, per a search snippet: [IS 7224 on archive.org](https://archive.org/download/gov.in.is.7224.2006/is.7224.2006.pdf)). Safe post wording: "unless the pack says iodised".
- With non-iodized salt at <2–5 mg/kg against Indian iodized salt at ≥15 ppm (mg/kg) at retail, switching cuts iodine from salt by roughly an order of magnitude or more. This is a calculation from the figures above, not a published comparison.

### Gaps
- I could not open a full-text Indian study giving ppm values per salt type (sendha namak, kala namak, Himalayan pink) by brand. The Delhi 2026 and Mohali 2023 papers need checking on the page for sample sizes and numbers.
- No PubMed paper was found that measures iodine in kala namak alone.

## Q2. What do WHO, ICMR/NIN, FSSAI, NIDDCP, NIH ODS, Harvard/Mayo/Cleveland Clinic say?

### Takeaway
WHO recommends that all food-grade salt be iodized. NIH ODS says specialty salts are usually not iodized. India's FSSAI made iodisation mandatory for common (white) salt for direct consumption but explicitly exempted rock salt, black salt and Himalayan pink salt in 2019. So in India these salts can legally be sold without any iodine. Evidence strength: **well established** (regulatory and official guidance).

### Cited Findings
- **WHO (2014 guideline, still current on eLENA):** "all food-grade salt, used in household and food processing, should be fortified with iodine as a safe and effective strategy for the prevention and control of iodine deficiency disorders in populations living in stable and emergency settings." WHO also states that salt reduction and salt iodization are compatible because the iodine concentration can be adjusted. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [WHO Guideline 9789241507929 (2014)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241507929); [WHO eLENA: Salt iodization](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/salt-iodization)
- **FSSAI, Regulation 2.3.12, Food Safety and Standards (Prohibition and Restrictions on Sales) Regulations, 2011:** common salt may not be sold for direct human consumption or used as a food ingredient unless it is iodised. The proviso allows non-iodised common salt only for iodisation, iron fortification, animal use, preservation, medicines and industrial use, with proper labels. FSSAI's FAQ says "edible common salt" in food standards means iodised salt only. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [FSSAI FAQ Iodised Salt, 28 Jul 2021](https://mail.fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/FAQ_Iodised_Salt_28_07_2021.pdf); [FSSAI compendium, Prohibition & Restriction of Sales, 01 Apr 2025](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Comp_Prohibition%20and%20Restrcition%20of%20sales%20XI_01042025.pdf); [Gazette notification, 04 Jul 2019](https://agriexchange.apeda.gov.in/food_standards/ind_document/Gazette_Notification_Common_Salt_04_07_2019.pdf)
- **FSSAI exemption for traditional salts (FSSAI news brief, 16 Jul 2019):** FSSAI clarified that "traditional salts such as rock salt, black salt, Himalayan pink salt, etc. shall not be mandatorily iodised" and that the notification "is meant only for the common salt or white salt". [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY; key claim for an Indian post, verify on page] — [FSSAI news brief PDF, 16 Jul 2019](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/media/FSSAI_News_Salt_FNB_16_07_2019.pdf)
- **India's legal iodine level:** not less than 30 ppm at manufacture and not less than 15 ppm at retail/consumer level. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY, Dept of Consumer Affairs salt document] — [consumeraffairs.nic.in Salt.pdf](https://consumeraffairs.nic.in/sites/default/files/file-uploads/ctocpas/Salt.pdf)
- **NIH ODS:** specialty salts (sea, kosher, Himalayan, fleur de sel) "are not usually iodized". See Q1. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [NIH ODS Iodine fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)
- **Iodine Global Network:** has a blog post, "The rising popularity of Himalayan pink salt". The page could not be opened, so its content is not quoted. — [IGN](https://ign.org/latest/blog/the-rising-popularity-of-himalayan-pink-salt/)
- **McGill Office for Science and Society:** says pink Himalayan salt is not iodized ("Taking off the Rose Coloured Glasses: The Pink Himalayan Salt Grift"). This is a university science-communication piece written by a student contributor. Treat it as low-tier. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [McGill OSS](https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/student-contributors-health-and-nutrition/taking-rose-coloured-glasses-pink-himalayan-salt-grift)

### Inferences
- For an Indian audience the FSSAI exemption is the central point. Indian law requires white salt to be iodised but not sendha namak, kala namak or pink salt. A family that switches its daily salt to these loses the main iodine source the national programme relies on.

### Gaps
- No ICMR-NIN (Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024), NIDDCP, Harvard Nutrition Source, Mayo Clinic or Cleveland Clinic statement was retrieved. Harvard's page was blocked and I did not search the others for lack of budget. A follow-up should check ICMR-NIN DGI 2024 and the NIDDCP page on the MoHFW/DGHS site.
- The NIH ODS page date and the exact current RDA wording were not confirmed. From memory, adults need 150 mcg/day, pregnancy 220 and lactation 290, but this was not verified in this session and should not be cited until it is checked.

## Q3. Is there evidence that people using non-iodized "gourmet"/rock salts have lower iodine status?

### Takeaway
Yes, observational studies show it consistently. Pregnant women using rock salt or non-iodized salt have substantially lower urinary iodine (UIC). No study of Indian women switching to sendha/pink salt was found. Evidence strength: **moderate** (cross-sectional associations, not trials).

### Cited Findings
- **Turkey, Yozgat (2021):** Apaydın et al., *Turkish Journal of Medical Sciences* 51(2) (2021), "The effects of salt consumption habits on iodine status and thyroid functions during pregnancy". 306 first-trimester pregnant women: 76.8% used table (iodized) salt, 23.2% rock salt. Median UIC was 123.7 μg/L in table-salt users vs 70.9 μg/L in rock-salt users (P < 0.001). The authors concluded "use of rock salt is associated with low urinary iodine concentration in pregnant women and TSH values within the reference limits are not a good indicator for determining the iodine level." [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY; most directly relevant study, verify on page] — [Turk J Med Sci vol51 iss2 art49](https://journals.tubitak.gov.tr/medical/vol51/iss2/49); [PDF](https://aj.tubitak.gov.tr/medical/issues/sag-21-51-2/sag-51-2-49-2007-127.pdf)
- **China, Zhejiang (2019):** Wang X et al., *Nutrients* 2019;11(2):413. 2,642 pregnant women. Median UIC was 152.9 μg/L in inland iodized-salt users vs 102.2 μg/L in non-iodized users (p = 0.0012), and 130.2 vs 104.3 μg/L in coastal women (p = 0.011). Non-iodized salt use, coastal residence and low iodine knowledge were each independently associated with low UIC. Women who thought seafood was the best way to prevent iodine deficiency had higher odds of low UIC (OR 2.02, 95% CI 1.53–2.67). [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC6412776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6412776/)
- **Cambodia (2016):** iodine status was significantly lower among mothers and children in households not using iodized salt. [SEARCH SNIPPET ONLY] — [PMC4848647](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4848647/)
- **Slovenia (2016):** describes non-iodised salt as "a niche market which could pose a risk of inadequate iodine intake" for people who use only non-iodised salt. This is an inference from availability, not measured status. [VERIFIED on page] — [PMC4963910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4963910/)

### Inferences
- WHO's cut-off for adequate iodine in pregnancy is a median UIC of 150 μg/L. That figure is from my own background knowledge and was not verified this session, so verify it before using. On that cut-off, the rock-salt group in Turkey (70.9 μg/L) would be clearly insufficient.
- The Zhejiang "seafood" finding parallels a common Indian belief that "natural" foods or salts cover iodine. It is a reasonable but indirect analogy.

### Gaps
- No Indian study measured urinary iodine in households that switched to sendha namak or pink salt. Navratri and fasting use of sendha namak is short-term and unlikely to matter on its own, but I found no source on it.
- No US or Australian study linking gourmet-salt users to lower iodine status was found.
