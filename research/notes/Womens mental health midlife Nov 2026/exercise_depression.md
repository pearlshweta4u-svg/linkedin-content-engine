## Noetel et al., BMJ 2024: network meta-analysis of exercise for depression

### Takeaway
Across 218 RCTs (14,170 people with major depression), walking or jogging, yoga and strength training gave moderate reductions in depression compared with active controls. Strength training was g = -0.49, and its effects were larger in women. The authors call these "core treatments" to consider alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants. Certainty is LOW for walking or jogging and VERY LOW for every other modality, so the wording has to be cautious.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Noetel M, et al. "Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials." BMJ 2024;384:e075847. Published 14 Feb 2024. A correction was published as BMJ 2024;385:q1024 (28 May 2024). PMID 38355154, PMCID PMC10870815, DOI 10.1136/bmj-2023-075847. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Design: Bayesian arm-based multilevel network meta-analysis of "any randomised trial with exercise arms for participants meeting clinical cut-offs for major depression". It covered 218 studies, 495 arms and 14,170 participants. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Effects versus active controls, as Hedges' g with 95% credible intervals. A more negative value means a larger reduction in depression. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
  - Walking or jogging: g = -0.62 (-0.80 to -0.45) in the abstract. The results section gives -0.63 (-0.80 to -0.46).
  - Yoga: g = -0.55 (-0.73 to -0.36).
  - Strength training: g = -0.49 (-0.69 to -0.29).
  - Mixed aerobic exercise: g = -0.43 (-0.61 to -0.24).
  - Tai chi or qigong: g = -0.42 (-0.65 to -0.21).
  - Dance: g = -0.96 (-1.36 to -0.56), but from only 5 studies.
  - Comparators in the same network: CBT alone g = -0.55 (-0.75 to -0.37); SSRIs alone g = -0.26 (-0.50 to -0.01); exercise plus SSRIs g = -0.55 (-0.86 to -0.23); aerobic exercise plus psychotherapy g = -0.54 (-0.76 to -0.32).
- Acceptability: "Strength training and yoga appeared to be the most acceptable modalities." [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Intensity: effects were proportional to prescribed intensity. Light activity (such as walking or hatha yoga) gave g = -0.58 (-0.82 to -0.33) and vigorous exercise gave g = -0.74 (-1.10 to -0.38). Light activity still helped. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- **Sex (relevant to this audience):** strength training and cycling had larger effects in women than in men. Yoga, tai chi, and aerobic exercise with psychotherapy had larger effects in men. These are study-level comparisons, not individual-level analyses. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Age: yoga, and aerobic exercise with psychotherapy, appeared more effective in older participants. Strength training appeared more effective in younger participants. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Certainty: evidence was graded with CINeMA, the GRADE-based tool for network meta-analyses. Confidence was **low for walking or jogging and very low for all other modalities**, including strength training and yoga. Only one study met Cochrane low risk-of-bias criteria. The results "appeared robust to publication bias". [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Conclusion (exact wording): "Exercise is an effective treatment for depression, with walking or jogging, yoga, and strength training more effective than other exercises, particularly when intense." The authors add: "These forms of exercise could be considered alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants as core treatments for depression." [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Comparison with CBT and SSRIs: the paper says exercise effects "seem comparable" to CBT, although confidence is lower, and that some forms of exercise had stronger effects than SSRIs alone. [VERIFIED-PAGE; paraphrased by the fetch tool, so check the exact sentence before quoting] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- A non-peer-reviewed patient forum (s4me) has criticised the paper's data reporting. It is not a credible source to cite, but it shows the paper is debated. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [s4me thread](https://s4me.info/threads/effect-of-exercise-for-depression-systematic-review-and-network-meta-analysis-of-randomised-controlled-trials-2024-noetel-et-al.37611)

### Inferences
- Safe phrasing: "A 2024 BMJ review of 218 trials found walking, yoga and strength training reduced depression symptoms, with effects in the same range as therapy. The certainty of that evidence is still low." Do not write "as good as antidepressants" or "better than medication". The SSRI comparison rests on few arms, and the CLAUDE.md rule against discouraging medication applies.
- The finding that strength training helped women more is useful for a strength coach. Present it as a signal from study-level data, not a proven sex difference.
- The trials enrolled people meeting clinical cut-offs for major depression. Applying the results to everyday low mood is an extrapolation.

- There is no midlife or menopause subgroup.

## Gordon et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2018: resistance training and depressive symptoms, plus newer resistance-training evidence

### Takeaway
A meta-analysis of 33 RCTs (1,877 participants) found resistance training gave a moderate reduction in depressive symptoms (Δ = 0.66). The benefit did not depend on training volume, health status or strength gains. A companion 2017 meta-analysis found a smaller reduction in anxiety (Δ = 0.31). Neither primary page could be opened in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Gordon BR, McDowell CP, Hallgren M, Meyer JD, Lyons M, Herring MP. "Association of Efficacy of Resistance Exercise Training With Depressive Symptoms: Meta-analysis and Meta-regression Analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials." JAMA Psychiatry 2018;75(6):566-576, DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.0572. Published online May 2018. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Iowa State repository](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/678d2a9c-4a2d-4cb3-9170-807faced6055); [Psychiatric News alert](https://alert.psychnews.org/2018/05/resistance-exercise-training-may-reduce.html)
- Results: 33 RCTs with 1,877 participants. Resistance training gave a significant reduction in depressive symptoms, with a moderate mean effect of Δ = 0.66 (95% CI 0.48-0.83; z = 7.35; P < .001). [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/psychiatry/depression/news/online/%7B9becd242-bd2c-4fb0-ba9b-db08f215bd18%7D/resistance-exercise-training-reduces-depressive-symptoms)
- Moderators: total prescribed volume, health status and strength improvement were not significantly associated with the antidepressant effect. Trials with blinded allocation or assessment showed smaller reductions. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/psychiatry/depression/news/online/%7B9becd242-bd2c-4fb0-ba9b-db08f215bd18%7D/resistance-exercise-training-reduces-depressive-symptoms)
- Larger effects in people with mild-to-moderate depression, and an NNT of about 4, are often reported for this paper. **I did not see either in any result here. Do not use them.**
- Companion anxiety paper: Gordon BR, et al. "The Effects of Resistance Exercise Training on Anxiety: A Meta-Analysis and Meta-Regression Analysis." Sports Medicine 2017 (PMID 28819746). It pooled 16 articles, 31 effects and 922 participants. Resistance training reduced anxiety with Δ = 0.31 (95% CI 0.17-0.44). Effects were larger in healthy participants (Δ = 0.50) than in people with a physical or mental illness (Δ = 0.19). Effects did not vary significantly by sex, age, intensity or strength gains. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [University of Limerick record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effects-of-resistance-exercise-training-on-anxiety-a-meta-ana/)
- Newer evidence: in the Noetel 2024 network meta-analysis, strength training against active controls gave g = -0.49 (-0.69 to -0.29), with very low certainty and larger effects in women. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Newer evidence: in the Singh 2023 umbrella review, strength-based activity had the largest median effect on depression (-0.64). For anxiety it had one of the smallest (-0.23). [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)

### Inferences
- Resistance training seems to help even when strength gains are modest, which suggests beginners can benefit. This rests on unverified snippets, so word it softly.
- For anxiety, the evidence favours yoga, mind-body and aerobic work over strength training. Singh found strength training's anxiety effect was the smallest, and Gordon 2017 found it small. Do not claim "lifting cures anxiety".

### Gaps
- I could not open the Gordon 2018 primary page (JAMA, PubMed and gwern were all blocked). The exact Key Points wording, the NNT and the sex moderator are unverified.
- I found no reliable post-2024 meta-analysis specific to resistance training in this session.

## Singh et al., Br J Sports Med 2023: umbrella review of physical activity for depression, anxiety and distress

### Takeaway
An umbrella review of 97 systematic reviews (1,039 trials, 128,119 adults) found medium reductions in depression (median -0.43) and anxiety (median -0.42) compared with usual care. Effects were larger at higher intensity and in interventions of 12 weeks or less. The authors say physical activity "should be a mainstay approach". Most of the included reviews were rated "critically low" quality on AMSTAR-2.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Singh B, Olds T, Curtis R, et al. "Effectiveness of physical activity interventions for improving depression, anxiety and distress: an overview of systematic reviews." Br J Sports Med 2023;57(18):1203-1209. Published online 16 Feb 2023 (accepted 3 Feb 2023; issue Sept 2023). DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2022-106195, PMCID PMC10579187, PROSPERO CRD42021292710. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Scale: "Ninety-seven reviews (1039 trials and 128 119 participants) were included." Searches ran from inception to 1 Jan 2022. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Effects compared with usual care: [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
  - Depression: median effect size -0.43 (IQR -0.66 to -0.27), from 72 meta-analyses.
  - Anxiety: median -0.42 (IQR -0.66 to -0.26), from 28 meta-analyses.
  - Psychological distress: -0.60 (95% CI -0.78 to -0.42), but from a single review of 6 RCTs (508 participants).
- Populations with the largest benefits: "people with depression, HIV and kidney disease, in pregnant and postpartum women, and in healthy individuals". Depression medians were -0.59 in pregnancy and -0.49 postpartum. There were **no perimenopause or menopause subgroups**. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Intensity: "Higher intensity physical activity was associated with greater improvements in symptoms." Depression medians were -0.22 for low, -0.56 for moderate and -0.70 for high intensity. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Duration: "Effectiveness of physical activity interventions diminished with longer duration interventions." Depression medians were -0.84 for 12 weeks or less, -0.46 for 12 to 23 weeks and -0.28 for 24 weeks or more. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Mode, depression medians: strength-based -0.64, mixed -0.47, mind-body (yoga or stretching) -0.46, aerobic -0.45. Mode, anxiety medians: mind-body -0.42, mixed -0.35, aerobic -0.29, strength -0.23. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Quality: 77 of the 97 reviews were rated "critically low" on AMSTAR-2, 10 low and 10 high. There was no evidence of publication bias. Most of the evidence concerns mild-to-moderate symptoms. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Comparison: the depression and anxiety effects are "comparable to or slightly greater than" those reported for psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy (SMD range -0.22 to -0.37). The authors call for head-to-head trials. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Conclusion: "Physical activity should be a mainstay approach in the management of depression, anxiety and psychological distress." [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Press-coverage distortions to avoid: "1.5 times more effective than medicines" came from the university press release, and "43% reduction" misreads an SMD as a percentage. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [UniSA release](https://www.unisa.edu.au/media-centre/Releases/2023/exercise-more-effective-than-medicines-to-manage-mental-health); [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/primary-care/20230301/umbrella-review-all-types-of-exercise-help-mental-health-symptoms)

### Inferences
- Do not write "exercise beats medication" or "1.5x more effective". Say "effects similar in size to therapy or medication, in mostly mild-to-moderate cases".
- The finding that interventions of 12 weeks or less had the largest effects probably reflects adherence and study design rather than benefits fading. That is my inference.

### Gaps
- I did not read the last 44k characters of the PMC page (the discussion and limitations).

## Pearce et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2022: dose-response and depression risk (prevention)

### Takeaway
In 15 prospective cohort studies (191,130 people), half the recommended activity (about 75 min/week of brisk walking) was linked to an 18% lower risk of developing depression. Meeting the recommendation (about 150 min/week) was linked to a 25% lower risk, and going further added little. This is observational evidence: it shows association, not proven cause.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Pearce M, Garcia L, Abbas A, et al. "Association Between Physical Activity and Risk of Depression: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis." JAMA Psychiatry 2022;79(6):550-559. Published online 13 Apr 2022. DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2022.0609, PMID 35416941, PMCID PMC9008579. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Scale: "Fifteen studies comprising 191 130 participants and 2 110 588 person-years were included". There were 28,806 incident depression events, and about 64% of participants were women. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Association: an inverse curvilinear association, with the steepest gains at low activity volumes. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Risk of depression by activity level: [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
  - 4.4 mMET-h/week (half the recommended volume): RR 0.82 (95% CI 0.77-0.87), about 18% lower risk.
  - 8.8 mMET-h/week (the recommended volume, about 150 min/week of moderate activity such as brisk walking): RR 0.75 (0.68-0.82), about 25% lower risk.
  - 17.5 mMET-h/week: RR 0.72 (0.64-0.81), from Table 2, showing little extra benefit.
- Preventable fraction: "11.5% (95% CI, 7.7%-15.4%) of depression cases could have been prevented" if less active adults had met the recommendations, assuming the association is causal. That is about 1 in 9 cases. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Conclusion: there are "significant mental health benefits from being physically active, even at levels below the public health recommendations." [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Sex: no sex-stratified analysis was run because only two sets of sex-stratified results were available. Meta-regression found the sex mix did not explain differences between studies. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)

### Inferences
- "About 75 minutes a week" as the equivalent of 4.4 mMET-h is a derivation: half of 150 min of moderate activity. The paper defines the dose in mMET-hours. Say "roughly half the recommended 150 minutes".
- Use "linked to" or "associated with", not "cuts your risk".

### Gaps
- There are no sex-specific or midlife-specific results, and none from Indian cohorts (the studies were mostly from high-income countries; check the full text if this is needed).

## Guidance: WHO, NHS, NICE

### Takeaway
NICE NG222 (June 2022) lists group exercise among the treatment options for a new episode of less severe depression, decided with the patient. Guided self-help is listed first. WHO says activity reduces depression and anxiety symptoms and recommends 150 to 300 min/week of moderate activity plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days. None of these pages could be opened in this session, so everything below is [SNIPPET-ONLY].

### Cited Findings
- NICE NG222, "Depression in adults: treatment and management", published 29 June 2022. Its treatment table for less severe depression includes **group exercise**, described as a physical activity programme led by a trained practitioner and designed for people with depression. It usually runs as more than 1 session a week for 10 weeks, with about 8 participants per group. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NICE NG222 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations)
- NICE rationale: there was "some evidence for the effectiveness of group mindfulness and meditation, group exercise, interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) and antidepressants and they were also cost effective so these were recommended as alternative treatments". Guided self-help sits at the top of the list. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NICE NG222 rationale](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG222/chapter/rationale-and-impact)
- **Wording caution:** the brief says NICE made group exercise "a first-line option". That is broadly fair: it is one of the first-line treatment options listed for less severe depression, but it is not the preferred first choice. Write "NICE lists group exercise among first-line treatment options for less severe depression". NICE also has a patient-decision resource. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NICE aid via NHS Lothian](https://apps.nhslothian.scot/files/sites/2/NICE-discussing-firstline-treatments-for-less-severe-depression-pdf.pdf)
- WHO 2020 guidelines on physical activity: regular activity can reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety. Adults should do at least 150 to 300 min/week of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 min vigorous) and muscle strengthening on 2 or more days a week. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [WHO guidelines at a glance](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789240014886)
- NHS: the "Exercise for depression" page was not reachable and no snippet was obtained. [Not verified] — [NHS page](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/guides-tools-and-activities/exercise-for-depression/)

### Inferences
- Guidance supports framing exercise as one evidence-based option within care, not a substitute for it.

## Women and midlife women specifically

### Takeaway
The direct evidence in midlife women is from smaller, heterogeneous meta-analyses. They consistently show exercise reduces depressive symptoms in peri- and postmenopausal women, but the certainty is very low to moderate. All of it is [SNIPPET-ONLY].

### Cited Findings
- Noetel 2024: strength training's effect on depression was larger in women than in men (study-level comparison). [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Pearce 2022: about 64% of participants were women, and there was no sex difference in meta-regression. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)
- Singh 2023: benefits were seen in pregnant (-0.59) and postpartum (-0.49) women. There was no menopause subgroup. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- BMC Public Health 2024 network meta-analysis in **postmenopausal women**: the pairwise effect on depression was SMD -0.71 (95% CI -0.94 to -0.48; I² = 78%). Mind-body, aerobic and multicomponent exercise all reduced depression. Evidence quality was very low to moderate. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [BMC Public Health, doi 10.1186/s12889-024-19348-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-024-19348-2)
- Li et al. 2025, Frontiers in Psychiatry, **menopausal women**: exercise reduced depressive symptoms, SMD -1.04 (95% CI -1.46 to -0.63). [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1641082/full)
- One network meta-analysis ranked aerobic exercise highest for peri- and postmenopausal women (SUCRA 78.7%). Another 2025 network meta-analysis in postmenopausal women reported that resistance and mind-body exercise showed the most potential. I could not confirm which papers these are. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1480623/pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1743949/full)

### Inferences
- Large SMDs (-0.7 to -1.0) in small menopause meta-analyses with high heterogeneity are likely to be inflated. Use the Noetel and Singh figures as the headline numbers and the menopause papers only as supporting evidence that "studies in menopausal women point the same way".
- I found no Indian-specific RCT evidence.

### Gaps
- None of the menopause papers' full texts were read. Author lists, trial counts and risk of bias are unverified.

## Caveats and how to describe evidence strength (no medication discouragement)

### Takeaway
Present exercise as an effective, evidence-based addition to care. Be clear that certainty is low, that most trials involve mild-to-moderate depression, and that anyone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm needs professional help. Never suggest stopping or avoiding medication.

### Cited Findings
- Noetel's own conclusion places exercise "alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants". Exercise plus SSRIs (g = -0.55) did better than SSRIs alone (g = -0.26), which supports combining them rather than replacing medication. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Singh: most of the evidence covers mild-to-moderate symptoms, and 77 of 97 reviews were of critically low quality. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)
- Noetel: certainty is low for walking or jogging and very low for strength training and yoga, and only one trial was at low risk of bias. Blinding is impossible in exercise trials, so expectancy effects are possible. [VERIFIED-PAGE] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)
- Gordon 2018: blinded trials showed smaller effects. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/psychiatry/depression/news/online/%7B9becd242-bd2c-4fb0-ba9b-db08f215bd18%7D/resistance-exercise-training-reduces-depressive-symptoms)

### Inferences
- Suggested evidence-strength wording:
  - "Strong and consistent evidence from many trials that exercise reduces depressive symptoms, though individual studies are often low quality."
  - "Walking, yoga and strength training showed the clearest effects (low-certainty evidence)."
  - "Linked to lower risk of developing depression (observational)."
- Suggested safety line: "If you're on medication, keep taking it. Exercise works alongside treatment. If low mood is severe, lasts more than two weeks, or comes with thoughts of self-harm, please speak to a doctor."
- In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is the government mental health helpline. I did not verify it in this session, so check it before including it.

### Gaps
- I did not verify NHS or NICE text on continuing medication, or the Tele-MANAS number.
