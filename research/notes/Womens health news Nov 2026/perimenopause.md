## Any new studies or guidance on perimenopause symptoms, HRT/MHT, or non-hormonal options in this window?

### Takeaway
Five verifiable items fall in the window. Two come from India: an RCT in Menopause (Belagavi, 24 Nov), where Hatha yoga matched or beat HIIT for muscle health, and an Indian expert review of MHT for bone health (Arch Osteoporos, 27 Oct). The other three are a Flo/Mayo study in Menopause (24 Nov) linking higher BMI to worse perimenopause symptoms, a European Society of Gynecology non-hormonal statement (18 Nov), and a large US records study (8 Nov) linking MHT to fewer shoulder problems. The strongest base for a checklist is the ESG statement together with the Indian yoga RCT.

### Cited Findings

**1. Hatha yoga vs HIIT for muscle and body composition, Indian RCT**
- Published online 24 Nov 2026 in *Menopause* (journal of The Menopause Society). DOI 10.1097/GME.0000000000002939, PMID 43075991. Authors are from the KLE Institute of Physiotherapy, Belagavi, Karnataka, India. Conflicts: "None reported." — [PubMed 43075991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075991/)
- Design: open-label, randomised, assessor-blinded parallel-arm trial. 161 postmenopausal women aged 46-70 were randomised; 134 completed (HIIT 69, yoga 65). Both groups did supervised group sessions 5 times a week for 12 weeks. Sarcopenia was diagnosed with Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia criteria. — [PubMed 43075991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075991/)
- Findings: both programmes improved all outcomes from baseline. In the intention-to-treat analysis (n=161), the groups differed only in gait speed, in favour of yoga (P=0.007). Handgrip, ASMI and body composition did not differ. In the complete-case analysis (n=134), yoga beat HIIT on handgrip strength, gait speed, appendicular muscle index, skeletal muscle mass and lean mass. Fat mass and body-fat % did not differ. The authors conclude "Either modality can be safely prescribed for postmenopausal women, with HY providing additional muscle health benefits." — [PubMed 43075991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075991/)
- Evidence strength: moderate. It is a single-centre RCT, short (12 weeks) and open-label, and most of the advantage for yoga appears only in the complete-case analysis. The participants are postmenopausal and 46-70, so the results apply to the upper end of the 35-50 audience.

**2. Higher BMI and worse perimenopause symptoms (Flo app + Mayo Clinic)**
- Published online 24 Nov 2026 in *Menopause*. DOI 10.1097/GME.0000000000002941, PMID 43075998. Authors are from Mayo Clinic (including Stephanie Faubion) and Flo Health Inc. — [PubMed 43075998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075998/)
- Design: cross-sectional survey of 1,988 perimenopausal women aged 35 or older, run in the Flo app from Dec 2024 to May 2025, using the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS). — [PubMed 43075998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075998/)
- Findings: after adjusting for age, ethnicity, income, education, smoking, activity, alcohol and comorbidities, obesity classes I-III were linked to higher total MRS scores (B=1.21-1.42) and higher somatic scores (B=0.76-0.92). BMI was not linked to psychological or urogenital scores. Anxiety and depression were the strongest predictors of symptom burden in every domain, followed by low income, lower education and smoking. More frequent physical activity was linked to lower symptom burden. — [PubMed 43075998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075998/)
- Evidence strength: low to moderate. The study is cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause. The sample is self-selected app users and BMI is probably self-reported. A WebSearch summary described a Flo press release saying 97 countries were covered; I could not open that page, so treat the figure as unverified.

**3. European Society of Gynecology statement on non-hormonal management**
- Published online 18 Nov 2026 in *Gynecological Endocrinology* (issue dated 31 Dec 2026). DOI 10.1080/09513590.2026.2751783, PMID 43049482. — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- Design: an ESG expert panel reviewed MEDLINE and EMBASE (guidelines, systematic reviews, RCTs) and reached its recommendations through a structured consensus process. — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- Findings: MHT remains the most effective treatment. Lifestyle (diet, physical activity, weight control, sleep hygiene, behavioural approaches) is described as "the foundation." For hot flushes, SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentin, clonidine, oxybutynin and pregabalin show "varying degrees of efficacy." The neurokinin antagonists fezolinetant and elinzanetant are "the most effective evidence-based non-hormonal pharmacologic therapies." CBT and clinical hypnosis reduce distress from hot flushes and improve sleep. Evidence for mindfulness, yoga, acupuncture, phytoestrogens, S-equol, pollen extracts and black cohosh ranges from moderate to limited. For vaginal and urinary symptoms (GSM), lubricants, moisturisers, hyaluronic acid and pelvic floor training help, but "current evidence does not support routine use of vaginal laser therapy." — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- Evidence strength: moderate. It is an expert consensus statement built on a literature review, not a GRADE systematic review. It is European, so drug availability in India will differ.

**4. MHT and lower odds of shoulder soft-tissue disorders (frozen shoulder, tendinitis, rotator cuff)**
- Published online 8 Nov 2026 in *Arthroscopy, Sports Medicine, and Rehabilitation*. DOI 10.1002/ars2.70158, PMID 43005556. — [PubMed 43005556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43005556/)
- Design: retrospective propensity-matched analysis of the TriNetX US database. 296,992 women aged 40-60 with a menopausal-state code were included; mean age was 49.1 and 4.1% were Asian. Level III evidence. — [PubMed 43005556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43005556/)
- Findings: systemic MHT users had modestly lower odds of adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) (OR 0.875), bicipital tendinitis (OR 0.775), calcific tendinitis (OR 0.829), impingement (OR 0.873) and rotator cuff tear (OR 0.892). Topical (transdermal) estrogen was more closely linked to lower odds than oral forms. — [PubMed 43005556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43005556/)
- Evidence strength: low. The study is observational and based on billing codes, the effect sizes are small (about 10-20% lower odds), and confounding is likely because women who get MHT differ from those who don't. It suggests an association and should not be used to recommend MHT for shoulders.

### Inferences
- The yoga RCT is the most locally relevant and most "save-worthy" item: Indian women, an Asian sarcopenia definition, and an activity that is cheap and widely available. It supports a message along the lines of "structured strength-focused movement 5x/week; yoga counts." It does not show that yoga beats weight training, because HIIT is not resistance training.
- The Flo study and the ESG statement agree that activity, weight, sleep and mental health matter for symptom burden. In the Flo data, anxiety and depression were stronger predictors than BMI. This supports a post on "screen your mood too" rather than one that says weight is the main cause.

### Cited Findings

**6. MenoMEC, US Delphi consensus on hormone vs non-hormone treatment for hot flushes**
- Published online 24 Nov 2026 in *Menopause*. DOI 10.1097/GME.0000000000002947, PMID 43076382. Authors include JoAnn Pinkerton, Lisa Larkin, Chrisandra Shufelt and Mary Jane Minkin. — [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/)
- Design: modified Delphi with 15 US experts; consensus meant 70% or more agreement. — [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/)
- Findings: hormone therapy is first-line for eligible women. Non-hormone options are for women with contraindications or who decline HT. First-line non-hormone options are SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and oxybutynin. Neurokinin drugs (fezolinetant, elinzanetant) are supported as first-line but "limited by access and cost." Clonidine and "most herbal/over-the-counter products were not recommended." The tool gives specific guidance for cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, thrombosis, liver disease, smoking and hormone-sensitive cancers. — [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/)
- Conflict of interest: the study was funded through Curta Inc. with money from Bayer, which markets elinzanetant. Panelists received Bayer honoraria, and the lead author later joined Bayer. — [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/)
- Evidence strength: expert opinion (Delphi), with industry funding. It is US-focused and is not an official Menopause Society position statement.
- Note: the ESG statement (item 3) and MenoMEC disagree on clonidine. ESG lists it among agents with "varying degrees of efficacy", while MenoMEC does not recommend it. — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/); [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/)

**7. Updated cardiovascular risk assessment before hormone therapy (PREVENT calculator + 2026 lipid guideline)**
- Published online 17 Nov 2026 in *Menopause*. DOI 10.1097/GME.0000000000002953, PMID 43042547. It is a perspective/framework piece. — [PubMed 43042547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43042547/)
- Content: the paper treats cardiovascular risk assessment as "an integral component of menopause management." It recommends the PREVENT calculator, which gives 10- and 30-year risk for ages 30-79 and includes kidney and metabolic factors, in line with the 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety dyslipidaemia guideline. It discusses coronary artery calcium scoring, female-specific risk enhancers, and how risk should guide whether to start HT and by which route. — [PubMed 43042547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43042547/)
- Evidence strength: expert framework, not new data. PREVENT has not been validated in Indian populations (my inference; the abstract does not address it).

**8. Indian expert review: MHT for bone health in India, 2026**
- Published 27 Oct 2026 in *Archives of Osteoporosis* 21(1):152. DOI 10.1007/s11657-026-01792-9, PMID 42946919. The 27 authors are Indian endocrinologists, including Ambrish Mithal, Sanjay Kalra, Nikhil Tandon, Sanjay Bhadada and Thomas Paul; the first affiliation is Max Super Specialty Hospital, New Delhi. — [PubMed 42946919](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42946919/)
- Content: Indian women reach menopause earlier than Western women, which raises their lifetime fracture risk. The review reports consistent evidence that MHT preserves bone density and reduces vertebral and hip fractures, especially when started early after menopause. It says post-WHI analyses support a more favourable benefit-risk profile in appropriately selected younger postmenopausal women, and that individualised MHT can improve bone outcomes alongside symptoms and quality of life. — [PubMed 42946919](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42946919/)
- Evidence strength: narrative expert review, not a guideline. It is still the most India-specific in-window item.

### Inferences
- Taken together, items 6-8 support a "questions to ask your doctor" post: ask whether you are a candidate for hormone therapy; ask what your heart-risk and bone-risk numbers are; ask which non-hormonal options exist if HT isn't for you. Any post that cites MenoMEC should mention the Bayer funding.

## What practical advice follows?

### Takeaway
The verified items support a short, evidence-based checklist built around movement, mood screening, a heart and bone risk check, and an informed conversation about hormonal and non-hormonal options. Nothing in the window justifies supplement or herbal recommendations; both consensus papers rate most herbal and OTC products as weak or not recommended.

### Cited Findings
- Do supervised strength or posture-based exercise about 5 times a week. Hatha yoga improved handgrip, muscle mass and gait speed in Indian postmenopausal women over 12 weeks, and HIIT also helped. — [PubMed 43075991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075991/)
- Physical activity was associated with lower perimenopause symptom burden, and obesity with higher physical symptom burden. — [PubMed 43075998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075998/)
- Tell your doctor about anxiety or low mood. These were the strongest predictors of symptom burden. — [PubMed 43075998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43075998/)
- Lifestyle (diet, activity, weight, sleep hygiene) is the foundation. CBT and clinical hypnosis have evidence for reducing distress from hot flushes and improving sleep. — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- For vaginal dryness, lubricants, moisturisers, hyaluronic acid and pelvic floor training help. Routine vaginal laser is not supported. — [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- Ask whether hormone therapy suits you. It is first-line for eligible women, and SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, oxybutynin or neurokinin drugs are options when HT isn't suitable. Most herbal/OTC products are not recommended. — [PubMed 43076382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076382/) (Bayer-funded); [PubMed 43049482](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049482/)
- Ask for a heart-risk assessment (BP, lipids, glucose, kidney function) before deciding on HT and its route. — [PubMed 43042547](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43042547/)
- Indian women reach menopause earlier and face higher lifetime fracture risk, so ask about bone health early. MHT started early after menopause protects bone in suitable women. — [PubMed 42946919](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42946919/)

### Inferences
- Save-worthy formats: (a) "5 questions to ask your gynaecologist in your 40s" (HT eligibility, heart risk, bone risk, mood, non-hormonal options); (b) "Yoga counts as strength work: what an Indian trial found"; (c) "Vaginal dryness: what evidence supports and what it doesn't (laser)."
- Per the CLAUDE.md health rules: no dosing, frame decisions as "ask your doctor," never discourage medication, and state evidence strength (RCT vs observational vs expert consensus).

### Gaps
- No in-window evidence specifically on heavy menstrual bleeding, cravings, joint pain treatment, or brain fog interventions in women aged 35-50. The cognition items are measurement and association studies only.
- The full texts were not read, only abstracts, so effect sizes beyond those quoted and the details of subgroup analyses are unverified.
