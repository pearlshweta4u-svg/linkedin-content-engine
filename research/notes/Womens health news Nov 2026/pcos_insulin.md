## Q1. New PCOS guideline updates, ESHRE/Endocrine Society statements or major PCOS studies in the window?

### Takeaway
I found no new guideline or society statement dated inside the window. The biggest PCOS news of 2026, the renaming to polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), came out in May 2026, so it is background only. One verified in-window PCOS paper is a modelling study (Int J Women's Health, 11 Nov 2026) estimating extra type 2 diabetes in women with PMOS. It repeats what current guidance says: every woman with PMOS should have her blood sugar checked, and the check repeated every 1-3 years.

### Cited Findings
**Development 1 (in window, verified on the page). Global modelled excess type 2 diabetes in women with PMOS, 1990-2023**
- Title: "Changing Global Modelled Excess Incidence of Type 2 Diabetes Among Women with Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome, 1990-2023: A Risk-Translation Study". Miao P, Jin Y, Lu J. *International Journal of Women's Health*, published 11 Nov 2026, 18:658542. DOI 10.2147/IJWH.S658542. Received 22 Sep 2026, accepted 31 Oct 2026. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- Evidence strength: computational modelling only, the weakest tier here. It applies a US cohort hazard ratio (HR 1.47) to GBD 2023 population data for women aged 20-49 in 204 countries. It is not new patient data and not a meta-analysis. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- Plain-words finding: the model puts PMOS-linked excess type 2 diabetes at about 78,000 new cases a year worldwide in 2023 (about 15.3 per 10,000 women with PMOS), up 178.6% since 1990. Most of the rise comes from population growth (41.3%) and rising background diabetes (34.9%). — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- India: India has the largest modelled case count, 13,373 excess cases a year, at a standardised rate of 19.9 per 10,000. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- Age pattern under the main assumption, excess cases per 10,000: about 12.0 at ages 30-34, 16.3 at 35-39 and 22.3 at 40-44, and roughly 30 at 45-49 (read from a figure). The authors warn that this pattern reverses under another cohort's assumptions. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- Totals are very sensitive to the assumed risk: other cohorts' hazard ratios give 168,734 (UK) up to 345,335 (Australia) cases. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- The paper restates the existing international guidance: assess blood sugar in all women with PMOS, reassess every 1-3 years depending on risk, and use the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) as the most accurate test whatever her BMI. The authors say their estimates should not change screening thresholds. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)

**Background (outside the window, so do not present it as new): PCOS renamed PMOS**
- Teede et al., *The Lancet* (2026; vol 407, pp 2329-2339, reported as May 2026), set out a global consensus renaming PCOS "polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)". Surveys of 14,360 patients and professionals plus Delphi rounds fed into the name. The 2023 international guideline text stays as it is, and the 2028 guideline will adopt PMOS. Seen in search results only; I did not open the Lancet or society pages. — [Endocrine Society press release](https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/2026/pcos-name-change); [Monash news](https://www.monash.edu/news/articles/polyendocrine-metabolic-ovarian-syndrome-new-name-to-improve-diagnosis-and-care-of-condition-affecting-170-million-women-worldwide); [PubMed 42119588](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42119588/)
- The in-window IJWH paper above confirms that the name was adopted in 2026 through a global consensus process (Teede et al., Lancet 2026). — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)

**Checked and excluded (outside the window)**
- AMH diagnostic-accuracy meta-analysis (J Korean Med Sci). It is printed as published 4 Aug 2026, though PMC search lists it under a 23 Nov 2026 collection date, so it is out of the window. Result: in adults AMH is "highly specific and moderately sensitive" (sensitivity 0.81, specificity 0.89; 83 studies, 33,376 participants) and should not be used alone. It is not accurate enough for teenagers. — [PMC13793648](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13793648)
- PCOS and diabetes/metabolic syndrome meta-analysis (J Ovarian Res), published 30 Jul 2026, out of the window. Diabetes RR 1.72 (95% CI 1.43-2.06) and metabolic syndrome RR 1.48, with very high heterogeneity and internal inconsistencies in its numbers. — [PMC13711723](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13711723)

### Inferences
- No new ESHRE, Endocrine Society or international PCOS guideline came out in the window. The current reference is still the 2023 international evidence-based guideline, with PMOS as the new name since May 2026.
- The 11 Nov paper is a modelling exercise. Use it only as context ("diabetes risk with PMOS is a growing global problem, and India has the most cases in absolute numbers"), never as proof of a specific risk figure for an individual woman.

## Q2. New studies or Indian news on insulin resistance, prediabetes or diabetes in women (e.g. World Diabetes Day, 14 Nov 2026)?

### Takeaway
One verified in-window Indian peer-reviewed study: BMJ Public Health, 12 Nov 2026, on opportunistic NCD screening of 24,289 adults at a Mumbai municipal hospital. About 1 in 6 screened positive on blood sugar, about two-thirds of those were in the prediabetic range, and women had far more central obesity. I could not verify any ICMR or PIB announcement for World Diabetes Day 2026 directly.

### Cited Findings
**Development 2 (in window, verified on the page). SPRINT-HD, Mumbai NCD-corner screening study**
- Title: "Screening Positivity Rate and RIsk AssessmeNT for Hypertension and Diabetes in Adults Visiting the Non-Communicable Disease (NCD) corners at a Tertiary Care Hospital in a Metropolitan City, India (SPRINT-HD): a cross-sectional study". Hadaye R, Bote MM, Vithalani N, Nair D. *BMJ Public Health*, published 12 Nov 2026, 4(4):e004099. DOI 10.1136/bmjph-2025-004099. Setting: TN Medical College & BYL Nair Hospital, Mumbai. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Evidence strength: observational, cross-sectional, hospital-based secondary analysis of programme data (Sep 2022 to Apr 2023). It is not a community prevalence estimate. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Sample: 24,289 analysed, 50.4% women (12,239). — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- 17.2% screened positive for diabetes (random capillary glucose >140 mg/dL), and 65.9% of these were in the prediabetic range. Among people who already knew they had diabetes, 34.8% were uncontrolled. Obesity was present in 54.2%. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Women: 14.1% of women had a high-risk waist size against 3.3% of men, and women had more clustered risk factors. Sex was not associated with diabetes positivity. Clustered risk factors were the strongest predictor overall. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Age: compared with under-30s, ages 40-49 had about 2.8 times the adjusted odds of screening positive for diabetes. Ages 30-39 were not significantly different (AOR 1.39). — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Caveats: the glucometer screen is not a diagnosis, so it needs confirmation. 12.1% of records were excluded. Some percentages in the discussion (24.1% elevated glucose) do not match the results section (17.2%), so cite the results-section figures. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- The authors call for stronger NCD programmes, lifestyle change for modifiable risk factors, and proper referral and follow-up. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)

## Q3. What practical lifestyle or testing advice follows?

### Takeaway
The verified in-window sources support a testing-focused checklist ("what to ask your doctor"). Neither supports new diet or supplement claims. Any lifestyle advice should rest on existing guidance, not on these papers.

### Cited Findings
- If you have PMOS/PCOS, international guidance says to get your blood sugar assessed whatever your weight, repeat it every 1-3 years depending on risk, and ask about a 75 g OGTT, which is the most accurate test. — [PMC13770460](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13770460)
- A random finger-prick sugar at a screening camp is only a first step. A positive result needs confirmation and follow-up, and many positives are in the prediabetic range, which can still be acted on. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- Waist size and clustered risk factors (blood pressure, weight, inactivity, family history) were the strongest signals in Mumbai adults, and women had much higher central obesity. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- If you already have a diabetes diagnosis, about a third of known diabetics in the Mumbai study were uncontrolled, so regular follow-up tests matter. — [PMC13775743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13775743)
- If a doctor mentions AMH for PCOS: in adults it is a helpful extra test, not a diagnosis on its own (the meta-analysis is outside the window and is background only). — [PMC13793648](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13793648)

### Inferences
- Possible save-worthy checklist, all of it backed by the sources above: (1) PCOS/PMOS diagnosis: ask for a blood sugar check, ideally an OGTT, even if you are slim; (2) repeat it every 1-3 years; (3) measure your waist, not just your weight; (4) a positive camp or glucometer result means book a confirmatory lab test; (5) from your 40s, screen regularly even without symptoms; (6) already diagnosed: keep up follow-up tests. No dosing or supplement claims; doctor-led.
- Evidence strength for the post: guideline-restated (OGTT advice) plus observational Indian data, with modelling only as context.

### Gaps
- No verified in-window RCT or meta-analysis on diet, exercise or supplements for insulin resistance in PCOS. The MDPI, DRCP and Frontiers leads listed under Q1 need to be opened before anyone uses them.
