# Exercise and screening evidence anchor the post

The strongest material for a 20 Nov 2026 post to Indian women aged 30-50 is the exercise-for-depression evidence and the PCOS screening guideline. Both were read on the source page (PMC full text). The 2024 BMJ network meta-analysis of 218 trials found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training cut depressive symptoms by moderate amounts, with strength training working better in women. India's own national survey, also page-verified, gives the local numbers: lifetime depression of 7.47% at age 40-49, current depression of 5.17% in large metros, and 80.1% of women with depression getting no treatment. The 2023 International PCOS Guideline makes a strong, high-certainty recommendation to screen all adults with PCOS for depression and anxiety. A 2018 JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis links autoimmune thyroiditis to about 3 times the odds of depression. The well-known "40% higher odds of depression in perimenopause" figure (Badawy 2024) could not be opened on any source page this session, and every perimenopause item rests on search snippets, so it needs a check against the abstract before it goes in the post. None of these sources supports "exercise beats medication". Each one places exercise, screening and hormone care alongside standard treatment, which fits the engine's rule never to discourage medication.

## How to read the verification labels

Researchers in this session could reach PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). They could not reach bmj.com, jamanetwork.com, nice.org.uk, nhs.uk, who.int, PubMed (cookie wall) or the UCL sites. **Page-verified** below means the claim was read on PMC full text or a PMC abstract. **Snippet-only** means it came from search-result summaries or secondary news coverage and must be checked on the primary page before it is quoted. This split runs by topic. The exercise core and the India, thyroid and PCOS core are mostly page-verified. The perimenopause section is snippet-only from start to finish.

## Strength training helps women most in 218 trials

The best single source is Noetel and colleagues' network meta-analysis in the BMJ, published **14 Feb 2024** (BMJ 2024;384:e075847, with a correction on 28 May 2024). It pooled **218 randomised trials, 495 arms and 14,170 participants** who met clinical cut-offs for major depression ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)). Compared with active controls, **walking or jogging gave g = -0.62, yoga -0.55 and strength training -0.49**. In the same network, CBT alone was -0.55 and SSRIs alone -0.26. The paper found that **strength training and cycling had larger effects in women than in men**, that strength training and yoga were the most acceptable forms of exercise, and that even light activity such as walking helped (g = -0.58) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10870815/)). Its conclusion, verified verbatim, reads: "These forms of exercise could be considered alongside psychotherapy and antidepressants as core treatments for depression." The wording in the post has to carry the weaknesses too. Certainty was **low for walking or jogging and very low for every other modality**, strength training included, and only one trial met Cochrane low risk-of-bias criteria. The sex difference comes from study-level comparisons, so it is a signal, not a proven difference. Exercise plus SSRIs (-0.55) outperformed SSRIs alone, which supports adding exercise to medication rather than replacing it.

Two more page-verified papers back this up. Singh et al.'s umbrella review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (online **16 Feb 2023**) covered **97 reviews, 1,039 trials and 128,119 adults**. It found median effects of **-0.43 for depression and -0.42 for anxiety** against usual care, and strength-based activity had the largest median depression effect (**-0.64**) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/)). For anxiety, strength work ranked lowest (-0.23) and mind-body work highest (-0.42), so the post should not claim that lifting fixes anxiety. Of the 97 reviews, 77 were rated "critically low" on AMSTAR-2, and the evidence mostly concerns mild-to-moderate symptoms. The university press release's "1.5 times more effective than medicines" line is a distortion, and so is reading the -0.43 effect as a "43% reduction". Neither should be repeated ([UniSA release](https://www.unisa.edu.au/media-centre/Releases/2023/exercise-more-effective-than-medicines-to-manage-mental-health)). For prevention, Pearce et al. in JAMA Psychiatry (**13 Apr 2022**) pooled **15 cohorts, 191,130 people, about 64% women**. Half the recommended activity volume (roughly 75 min a week of brisk walking, a derived figure) was linked to **18% lower risk** of developing depression (RR 0.82), the full recommended dose to **25% lower** (RR 0.75), and doing more added little ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9008579/)). This is observational, so the post should say "linked to" rather than "cuts".

Some exercise items are snippet-only. Gordon et al. (JAMA Psychiatry, May 2018) found resistance training reduced depressive symptoms across **33 RCTs and 1,877 participants (Δ = 0.66)**, regardless of training volume or strength gains, but blinded trials showed smaller effects ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/psychiatry/depression/news/online/%7B9becd242-bd2c-4fb0-ba9b-db08f215bd18%7D/resistance-exercise-training-reduces-depressive-symptoms)). Its companion anxiety meta-analysis reported a smaller effect, Δ = 0.31 ([University of Limerick](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effects-of-resistance-exercise-training-on-anxiety-a-meta-ana/)). An NNT of about 4 is often quoted for Gordon 2018, but it did not appear in any result here, so leave it out. Studies specific to menopause point the same way, with very low to moderate certainty: a BMC Public Health 2024 network meta-analysis in postmenopausal women reported SMD -0.71 ([BMC Public Health](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-024-19348-2)), and Li et al. 2025 reported SMD -1.04 in menopausal women ([Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1641082/full)). These small, heterogeneous pools probably overstate the effect. On guidance, NICE NG222 (29 June 2022) lists **group exercise among the first-line treatment options for less severe depression**, after guided self-help ([NICE NG222](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations)). WHO recommends 150-300 min of moderate activity a week plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days ([WHO](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/9789240014886)). Both are snippet-only.

## Perimenopause raises depression odds about 40%, unverified

The headline perimenopause figure comes from Badawy, Spector, Li and Desai in the Journal of Affective Disorders (2024;357:126-133, online April 2024). Their pooled analysis found perimenopausal women had **OR 1.40 (95% CI 1.21-1.61)** for depressive symptoms and diagnoses compared with premenopausal women, and **no significant increase after menopause** ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/mental-health/risk-for-depressive-symptoms-increased-for-perimenopausal-women); [UCL news, May 2024](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/may/women-are-40-more-likely-experience-depression-during-perimenopause)). The sample size is inconsistent across sources: search summaries of the abstract say 7 papers and 9,141 participants in the meta-analyses, while Pharmacy Times reports 11,965 ([Pharmacy Times](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/women-during-perimenopause-are-more-likely-to-experience-depression-study-finds)). The 40% is an odds ratio, not a relative risk. The authors could not adjust for prior depression, and the studies defined menopausal stage in different ways. **Everything in this section is snippet-only**, because the UCL hosts did not resolve and no other perimenopause page was fetched. Rated on the source's design, the evidence is moderate. The safe wording is "large reviews suggest the odds of depression rise by roughly 40% during perimenopause", and it should go in only after someone confirms OR 1.40 against the abstract (DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2024.04.041).

The older SWAN cohort agrees that risk rises during the transition. In 221 women at the Pittsburgh site, major depression was more likely during and just after the transition, and a past history of depression predicted it strongly ([PMC3584692](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3584692)). News coverage of another SWAN analysis reported 30% higher odds in early perimenopause and 73% higher in late perimenopause ([MDedge](https://mdedge.com/internalmedicinenews/article/19000/womens-health/risk-first-ever-depression-rises-during)), but those numbers were not traced to the primary paper. The NHS lists mood swings, low mood, anxiety and "problems with memory or concentration" as perimenopausal symptoms that can start years before periods stop ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/menopause/symptoms/)). That NHS wording is safer than "brain fog". Mayo Clinic notes that women with past PMS, PMDD or postpartum depression are more likely to have mood changes in perimenopause ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/perimenopause/symptoms-causes/syc-20354666)). All of these are snippet-only.

Treatment guidance lines up, again from snippets. The 2018 NAMS/NNDC guideline names **antidepressants and psychotherapy as first-line** for perimenopausal major depression, and says estrogen shows antidepressant effects mainly in women with hot flashes but is not approved for depression ([MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/guidelines-released-perimenopausal-depression); [NNDC](https://nndc.org/?p=8402)). NICE NG23, updated **7 Nov 2024**, says to consider HRT for low mood that began alongside other menopausal symptoms, and CBT for depressive symptoms linked to hot flushes, where neither meets criteria for a depression diagnosis ([NICE news](https://www.nice.org.uk/news/articles/updated-guideline-recommends-more-treatment-choices-for-menopause-symptoms)). Two Menopause Society reviews presented in October 2025 found hormone therapy **does not consistently reduce anxiety** ([Menopause Society](https://menopause.org/press-releases/feeling-anxious-during-menopause-hormone-therapy-may-or-may-not-help)). One factsheet that turned up in results claims antidepressants "do not help" menopausal mood changes. That misreads NICE and would discourage medication, so it must not be repeated. For a timely hook, The Menopause Society's 2026 meeting (**20-24 Oct 2026**, San Diego) ran a plenary titled "Mind the Gap: Mood Disorders, Suicide Risk, and the Menopause Transition" ([program PDF](https://menopause.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-TMS-Annual-Meeting-Program-15.pdf)). Only the title is confirmed, and no findings from the session were seen. A Medscape item on allopregnanolone levels from the same meeting surfaced once and could not be found again, so leave it out.

## India's survey shows a gap of 80% for women

The India numbers come from the National Mental Health Survey 2015-16 (NIMHANS), as analysed by Arvind, Gururaj et al. in BMJ Open on **27 Jun 2019**. The survey covered **34,802 adults in 12 states** and used structured MINI interviews, and the findings were read on PMC ([PMC6609075](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6609075/)). Current depression was **2.68%** overall and **3.0% in women**. Lifetime depression was **5.72% in women vs 4.75% in men**, and women's adjusted odds of current depression were **1.23 (1.04-1.45)**. By age, current depression peaked at **3.6% in the 40-59 band** and lifetime depression reached **7.47% at 40-49**. In metros of over a million people, current depression was **5.17% vs 2.15% in rural areas** (lifetime 8.23% vs 4.48%), which matters for an audience of urban working women. The **treatment gap was 80.1% for women** (79.1% overall). Among people with current depression, 67.28% said it affected their work life (Table 3). The abstract's "nearly two-thirds" is close but not identical, so cite Table 3. The figures are a decade old, and the post should say they come from the 2015-16 survey. The same survey found higher overall mental morbidity in men because of alcohol use, so the accurate line is "depression and anxiety disorders are more common in women", not "women have more mental illness" ([PARI library summary](https://ruralindiaonline.org/en/library/resource/national-mental-health-survey-of-india-2015-16-summary/)).

Newer India items are weaker. NMHS-2 (2025-26, all states and UTs, with women as a named focus group) had its national report "expected" on 10 Oct 2026 ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/karnataka/bengaluru/bengaluru-nimhans-hosts-conclave-on-mental-health-survey-3944857)). Its release was not confirmed and no NMHS-2 figures were found, so none should be cited. The GBD India paper in Lancet Psychiatry (2020) reports **45.7 million Indians with depressive disorders in 2017** and a higher burden in women ([PHFI copy](https://phfi.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Scientific-Paper-for-GBD-India-Mental-Disorders.pdf)), but only through snippets. A 2021 meta-analysis pooled depression prevalence in peri- and postmenopausal Indian women at **42.47%** with I² = 97.7% ([CAS catalogue record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/cmedm,33582315)). Single-site Indian studies range from 39% to 85% on screening scales. Heterogeneity this high makes those percentages unfit for a headline. For a helpline, Tele-MANAS had handled more than **18 lakh calls through 53 cells in 36 states/UTs in 20 languages** by Feb 2025, according to the government's News on AIR ([News on AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/over-18-lakh-calls-handled-by-tele-manas-73-crore-abha-ids-created-govt)). News reports of a Lok Sabha reply put the count at about 29.8 lakh by 27 Nov 2025 ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/amp/tech/new-delhi-over-298-lakh-calls-handled-by-tele-manas-cells-govt-strengthens-womens-mental-health-support)). Check the number 14416 on an official page before printing it.

## Thyroid and PCOS justify asking for a check

Siegmann et al. (JAMA Psychiatry, online **2 May 2018**) pooled **19 studies, 21 samples and 36,174 participants**. They found autoimmune thyroiditis linked to **OR 3.56 (2.14-5.94) for depression** and **OR 2.32 (1.40-3.85) for anxiety disorders**, with I² around 90% ([PMC6137529](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6137529/)). The PMC abstract is page-verified. The JAMA Key Points, possibly reflecting a 2019 correction, give 20 studies, 44,388 participants and a depression OR of 3.31, but that version is snippet-only. "About 3 times the odds of depression and about 2 times the odds of anxiety" holds for both versions. The authors recommend testing for thyroid autoimmunity in people with depression or anxiety, and screening people with thyroiditis for psychiatric symptoms. The evidence is moderate for association and low for causation, and funnel plots hint at publication bias. No NICE recommendation to test thyroid function in depression could be verified, so the post should not say "NICE says test your thyroid".

PCOS has the strongest guidance of any topic in this set. The 2023 International Evidence-based PCOS Guideline (Teede et al., JCEM, **15 Aug 2023**), led by Monash and co-sponsored by ESHRE, ASRM, the Endocrine Society and ESE, makes recommendation **2.2.1: screen for depression in all adults and adolescents with PCOS**, and **2.2.2: screen for anxiety in all adults**. Both are graded strong on high-certainty evidence ([PMC10505534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)). The guideline says psychological therapy "could be considered first-line" and antidepressants are used when disorders are "clearly documented and persistent". It also notes that lifestyle change and PCOS treatment can improve psychological symptoms. This is page-verified and supports medication where indicated. The best-known effect sizes come from Cooney et al. (Hum Reprod, May 2017), who found women with PCOS had **OR 4.18 for moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms** and **OR 6.55 for moderate-to-severe anxiety symptoms**, all from cross-sectional studies ([QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/28333286/high-prevalence-of-moderate-and-severe-depressive-and-anxiety-symptoms-in-polycystic-ovary-syndrome-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)). These are snippet-only. A newer pooled analysis quoted in coverage of the guideline gives lower odds, depression OR 2.2 and anxiety 1.65 ([MJA InSight+](https://insightplus.mja.com.au/2024/37/new-guideline-for-polycystic-ovary-syndrome-management)). So "around 2 to 4 times the odds" is the honest range. Neither topic has Indian-specific data in these notes.

## Conclusion

Together these sources support a post about midlife low mood that is better grounded than the usual "it's just hormones" message. Mood changes in the 30s and 40s have several plausible, testable contributors: the perimenopausal transition, thyroid autoimmunity and PCOS. Each of these has a reason to see a doctor attached, and regular movement, strength training especially, is a well-supported addition to whatever care follows. The page-verified core gives the post the Noetel and Singh exercise data, the NMHS numbers for metro women and the 80% treatment gap, the PCOS screening recommendation, and the thyroid odds ratios. The perimenopause 40% figure is the most quotable number in the set and also the least verified, so confirm it against the Badawy abstract or drop it.

Some gaps remain. No Indian trial of exercise for depression was found, there is no perimenopause subgroup in the big exercise reviews, no NMHS-2 data has been released, and no pooled estimate for perimenopausal anxiety exists. The post should name these limits rather than paper over them. A safety line is needed too: keep taking any prescribed medication, and see a doctor if low mood is severe, lasts more than two weeks or comes with thoughts of self-harm. Add Tele-MANAS once the number is confirmed.
