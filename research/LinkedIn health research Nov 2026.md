# Midlife pain and prediabetes anchor November posts

The strongest credible, in-window anchor for Thursday 12 November is the **International Menopause Society's World Menopause Day 2026 campaign (18 Oct 2026) on chronic pain at midlife**. It comes with a *Climacteric* white paper and a patient factsheet, and it reports that **about half of midlife women live with chronic pain**, often from more than one condition. That makes a natural "why does everything hurt after 40?" checklist post. Two other in-window items are usable as alternates with careful framing. The first is the **Indian double-fortified-salt debate** in The Hindu (27 Oct and 5 Nov 2026), which supports an "ask for ferritin and B12, not just Hb" post. The second is the **US FDA's 30 Oct 2026 removal of boxed warnings from three estradiol patches**, which is US-only and easily misread as "HRT is now safe". Nothing recent and credible was found inside 10 Oct to 9 Nov 2026 for **thyroid/Hashimoto's, PCOS, insulin resistance or gut health in women**. The big 2026 items on those topics are older: the PCOS-to-PMOS rename (May), the ATA pregnancy thyroid guideline (May) and the ICMR-SALT-T 1 iodine/thyroid study (17 Sep). For Friday 13 November, the prediabetes fact sheet holds up. **ICMR-INDIAB found 15.3% of Indian adults (about 136 million) have prediabetes**, more than the 11.4% (about 101 million) with diabetes. The ADA and WHO disagree on the fasting cut-off (100 vs 110 mg/dL). The 2023 PCOS guideline names the **75-g OGTT** as the best test. In the Indian prevention trial IDPP-1, **55% of untreated participants developed diabetes within three years**. One correction to the brief: the **World Diabetes Day 2026 theme is "Early action on diabetes" (#ActEarlyOnDiabetes)**, not "Diabetes and well-being". Every research note in this set was built from search-engine extracts, because the session's network allowlist blocked direct page fetches. **No figure below has been confirmed against a full primary text**, so all of it needs primary-source checking before posting, and the items flagged as high priority need it most.

## How to read the grades and verification flags

Each claim carries a date, a URL and an evidence grade. **A** means a randomised trial, an official guideline, or an official regulatory or organisational record (for a regulatory record, the grade covers the fact of the decision, not the safety science behind it). **B** means a large, nationally representative survey or a large cross-sectional study. **C** means an observational cohort, a meta-analysis of observational data, a society white paper or narrative review, or a conference abstract. **D** means secondary press, opinion or commentary only. Because the researchers could not open thelancet.com, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, diabetesjournals.org, imsociety.org, contemporaryobgyn.net, theprint.in or thesouthfirst.com in this session, every item is marked **"Verify"**. Items marked **"Verify: HIGH"** were seen only in a single secondary source, have conflicting values across sources, or have an unconfirmed date. Treat those as unusable until someone checks them.

Two documents accompany it. The first is a white paper, "Chronic pain at midlife", in *Climacteric*, by 10 international authors led by Angela Mailis, with DOI reported as 10.1080/13697137.2026.2722558 ([IMS white paper PDF](https://www.imsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/IMS-White-Paper-2026.pdf); [PubMed 42714958](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42714958/)). The second is a patient factsheet ([WMD26 factsheet PDF](https://www.imsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/WMD26-Factsheet.pdf)). Search extracts report four core messages. **Approximately half of midlife women experience chronic pain, often with more than one overlapping condition**, including fibromyalgia, migraine and chronic pelvic pain. Care should be individualised and multidisciplinary. Evidence that **menopause hormone therapy helps established chronic musculoskeletal pain is "limited and inconsistent"** ([The Well News](https://www.thewellnews.com/womens-issues/world-menopause-day-2026-taking-midlife-chronic-pain-seriously/)). Finally, **fibromyalgia is around three times more common in women than men** (factsheet). This is grade **C** evidence: an expert narrative review, not a trial or guideline. There is one dating wrinkle. The white paper sits in a "2026/09" upload folder, so it was probably online in September, while the campaign day itself falls inside the window. Frame the post as "this year's World Menopause Day focus", not "a new study". A "57 studies on HRT and musculoskeletal pain" figure circulating in secondary coverage is **unverified and should not be used**. For Indian women aged 30–50, the practical angle is a symptom checklist. Joint pain, migraine or pelvic pain that worsens in the 40s is not "just age". Track pain alongside cycle changes. Ask whether perimenopause is contributing. Build care around physiotherapy and strength work, sleep and mental health. Be wary of anyone selling HRT as a pain cure.

## Prediabetes outnumbers diabetes: 136 million Indians sit at the early-action stage

The national anchor is **ICMR-INDIAB-17**: Anjana RM et al., *Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol* 2023;11(7):474–489, published online June 2023, PMID 37301218 ([The Lancet D&E](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(23)00119-5/fulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301218/)). It reports weighted **diabetes prevalence of 11.4% (95% CI 10.2–12.5)** and **prediabetes prevalence of 15.3% (95% CI 13.9–16.6)**, equal to about **101 million and 136 million people**. The figures come from OGTT testing under WHO criteria, in **113,043 adults aged 20 and over across all states and UTs**, with glycaemic analysis on 107,119 people. Grade **B**. These headline numbers are concordant across the abstract snippet, the National Medical Journal of India ([NMJI](https://nmji.in/lessons-learnt-from-the-icmrindiab-study/)) and press. The data were **collected 2008–2020**, so "2023" is the publication year, not the measurement year. Urban diabetes prevalence was **16.4%**. The rural figure of 8.9% appears only secondhand, and another review gives 17.2% vs 9.4%, so it is **Verify: HIGH** against Lancet Table 2. Prediabetes **did not differ significantly between urban and rural areas**, and in many lower-HDI states prediabetes outnumbers diabetes, which signals a coming wave. For a women-focused post, note what the paper does *not* provide in any visible extract: **no national women-only prediabetes figure**. The abstract lists **male sex** among diabetes risk factors. So the honest headline is "136 million Indians", not "X million Indian women". Do not mix in the earlier 15-state ICMR-INDIAB figures from 2017 (7.3% diabetes, 10.3% prediabetes) ([Manipal portal](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/prevalence-of-diabetes-and-prediabetes-in-15-states-of-india-resu/)).

Women-specific data exist only at the edges. An NFHS-4 district analysis put age-adjusted **diabetes prevalence among women aged 35–49 at about 10.7%** ([PMC7270370](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7270370/); grade B, random capillary glucose, not comparable with OGTT). LASI data on adults 45+ reportedly show **prediabetes slightly more common in women than men (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.19)**. This was seen only through a 2026 Frontiers summary, without the underlying paper ([Frontiers in Public Health 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1766812/full); **Verify: HIGH**). If used, it applies to women 45 and over only, not to 30–44.

The cut-off contrast gives the post its most save-worthy line. The **ADA Standards of Care 2026** (*Diabetes Care* vol 49, Suppl 1, January 2026, Section 2) defines prediabetes as **HbA1c 5.7–6.4%, fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dL, or 2-h OGTT 140–199 mg/dL**. Diabetes is **HbA1c ≥6.5%, FPG ≥126, or 2-h ≥200 mg/dL** ([ADA SOC26](https://professionaleducation.diabetes.org/ProductInfo/SOC26); ranges confirmed via [Johns Hopkins Diabetes Guide](https://www.hopkinsguides.com/hopkins/view/Johns_Hopkins_Diabetes_Guide/547125/all/Prediabetes_or_Categories_of_Increased_Risk_for_Diabetes%C2%A0) and [iatroX](https://www.iatrox.com/clinical-guidance/us/prediabetes-type-2-diabetes-screening-diagnosis)). Grade **A**. The ranges have been stable for years, but check them against Table 2.5 of the 2026 edition. **WHO/IDF set impaired fasting glucose at 110–125 mg/dL** and do not endorse an HbA1c prediabetes range. The WHO/IDF 2006 primary document was not fetched; the figure came via the Hopkins guide. **Indian RSSDI recommendations have used 100–125 (2017, and RSSDI-ESI 2020, which adds HbA1c 5.7–6.4%)** ([RSSDI 2017 PDF](https://www.rssdi.in/newwebsite/pdfdata/rssdiGuidelines/2017/RSSDI%20clinical%20practice%20recommendations%20for%20the%20management%20of%20type%202%20DM-2017.pdf); [RSSDI-ESI 2020](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/indjem/fulltext/10.4103/ijem.ijem_225_20~rssdi-esi-clinical-practice-recommendations-for-the)). The 2022 edition reportedly uses 110–125 ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/367148390_RSSDI_Clinical_Practice_Recommendations_for_the_Management_of_Type_2_Diabetes_Mellitus_2022); **Verify: HIGH**). The practical consequence is concrete: **a fasting sugar of 105 mg/dL is "prediabetes" under ADA but "normal" under WHO**. Because ICMR-INDIAB used WHO criteria, the 136 million figure probably *understates* prediabetes by ADA standards. That last point is an inference until the paper's Methods are checked.

## PCOS is a diabetes risk signal, and the OGTT is the test to ask for

The **2023 International Evidence-based PCOS Guideline** (Teede HJ et al.) was published in August 2023 simultaneously in *Fertil Steril*, *Hum Reprod*, *JCEM* and *Eur J Endocrinol*, and **endorsed by 39 organisations**. It states that women with PCOS have **increased risk of impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes** ([Fertil Steril version, Cardiff ORCA PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/161859/1/PIIS0015028223007197.pdf); [ASRM PDF](https://www.asrm.org/globalassets/_asrm/practice-guidance/practice-guidelines/pdf/recommendations_from_the_2023_int_evidence-based_guideline_on_pcos.pdf); [PMC10755126](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10755126/)). Grade **A**. Its screening advice: assess glycaemic status **at diagnosis in all adults and adolescents with PCOS, then every 1–3 years** depending on risk factors. The **75-g OGTT is the most accurate test regardless of BMI**, with fasting glucose or HbA1c as fallbacks. An OGTT should be offered **when planning pregnancy or seeking fertility treatment**, before 20 weeks if not done preconception, and **at 24–28 weeks in all pregnant women with PCOS**. Check the exact wording, because one extract said "first prenatal visit" instead of "before 20 weeks". The guideline itself was not confirmed to state a risk ratio, so take risk sizes from the observational literature. A meta-analysis gives **RR 3.45 (95% CI 2.95–4.05)**, rising to **4.86 with obesity but still 1.62 in non-obese women** ([PMC10297688](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10297688/); grade C). A Danish nationwide cohort found **HR 4.0** over a median 11 years ([Rubin et al., JCEM 2017](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/10/3848/4096783); grade C). "Roughly 3–4 times the risk" is defensible if attributed to these studies rather than to the guideline. The post's action line is simple: if you have PCOS, ask for an OGTT, not just a fasting sugar. Note that PCOS was renamed PMOS in May 2026 ([Endocrine Society](https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/2026/pcos-name-change)), so mention both names.

## Prevention works, and the Indian trial shows the stakes

The **US Diabetes Prevention Program** (Knowler WC et al., *NEJM* 2002;346:393–403, 7 Feb 2002, PMID 11832527) randomised **3,234 people with elevated fasting and post-load glucose**, with mean follow-up of 2.8 years ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11832527/); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1370926/)). **Lifestyle change cut diabetes incidence by 58% (95% CI 48–66%) and metformin by 31% (17–43%)** compared with placebo. Incidence was 4.8, 7.8 and 11.0 per 100 person-years, and **6.9 people needed lifestyle intervention for three years to prevent one case**. Grade **A**. The benefit persisted but narrowed over time. At 15 years, incidence was 27% lower with lifestyle and 18% lower with metformin; the journal citation (*Lancet D&E* 2015;3:866–875) is **Verify: HIGH** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282132191_Long-term_effects_of_lifestyle_intervention_or_metformin_on_diabetes_development_and_microvascular_complications_over_15-year_follow-up_the_Diabetes_Prevention_Program_Outcomes_Study)). At **21 years** (*Lancet D&E*, online 28 Apr 2025, PMID 40311647), the HR was about 0.76 for lifestyle and 0.83 for metformin, with **median diabetes-free time extended by 3.5 and 2.5 years** respectively ([The Lancet D&E](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587%2825%2900022-1/abstract); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40311647/)). Effects reportedly held across sexes ([Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/826824)).

The **Indian Diabetes Prevention Programme (IDPP-1)** (Ramachandran A et al., *Diabetologia* 2006;49(2):289–297, DOI 10.1007/s00125-005-0097-z) studied Asian Indians with impaired glucose tolerance ([Helsinki research portal](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/the-indian-diabetes-prevention-programme-shows-that-lifestyle-mod/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7379354_The_Indian_Diabetes_Prevention_Programme_shows_that_lifestyle_modification_and_metformin_prevent_type_2_diabetes_in_Asian_Indian_subjects_with_impaired_glucose_tolerance_IDPP-1)). **Three-year cumulative incidence was 55.0% in controls**, against 39.3% with lifestyle, 40.5% with metformin and 39.5% with both. That gives **relative risk reductions of about 28.5%, 26.4% and 28.2%**, and **combining the two added nothing** ([PubMed 19587369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19587369)). Grade **A**. Sample size differs by source (502 completers vs 522 in a review table), so **Verify: HIGH** on n. Two framing points matter. First, the 55% control conversion is the most arresting Indian number in this fact sheet: without help, more than half of high-risk participants developed diabetes in three years. Second, the smaller relative effect than the US DPP (about 28% vs 58%) does not mean lifestyle "works less" in Indians. The Indian population was higher-risk, and the **absolute reduction was larger: 14.5 vs 7.2 cases per 100**.

**World Diabetes Day falls on Saturday 14 November 2026.** The **IDF's 2026–27 theme is "Early action on diabetes"**, with the hashtags **#ActEarlyOnDiabetes** and #WorldDiabetesDay. The campaign stresses that type 2 diabetes often has no early symptoms and urges regular testing, **especially after age 30**, or earlier for people with risk factors ([IDF, WDD 2026-27](https://idf.org/news-and-resources/news/wdd-2026-27/); [worlddiabetesday.org](https://worlddiabetesday.org/)). Grade **A** (official). WHO's 2026 campaign page also emphasises earlier diagnosis ([WHO](https://www.who.int/campaigns/world-diabetes-day/2026)). The older "Diabetes and well-being" theme (2024–2026) still appears on some secondary sites ([Dagens Diabetes](https://dagensdiabetes.se/?p=4210)) and is superseded. IDF and WHO agree, but neither page was opened directly, so quote the theme exactly once it is confirmed on idf.org.

## Conclusion

The window's real news on women's endocrine health is narrower than a weekly content calendar assumes. Only menopause produced credible, datable developments between 10 Oct and 9 Nov 2026. Even those are campaigns, conference talk and a US regulatory action rather than new trial data. The honest editorial move is to anchor Thursday on the IMS chronic-pain theme, which is credible, practical and culturally resonant. Run the anaemia "Hb isn't enough" angle as an Indian-policy alternative. Present PCOS, thyroid and insulin-resistance content openly as "2026 updates you may have missed" instead of inventing recency. Gut health in women has no usable recent anchor at all.

The two posts also connect. The IDF's "act early" theme, the PCOS guideline's OGTT advice and the 55% three-year conversion in IDPP-1 all point to one message: know your numbers and the test behind them. Together with the ADA-versus-WHO cut-off gap, that gives Indian women aged 30–50 something concrete to save, namely which test to ask for and what result should prompt action. The binding constraint is verification rather than content. Allowing the blocked hosts (thelancet.com, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, diabetesjournals.org, imsociety.org) and checking the "Verify: HIGH" items, especially the sex-specific ICMR-INDIAB tables, would turn a solid draft into publishable copy.
