# Pink salt quietly removes India's iodine safety net

Pink, Himalayan, rock (sendha) and black (kala) salt are not reliable sources of iodine. Every study in these notes that measured them found negligible or undetectable iodine. Indian iodized salt, by law, carries at least 15 ppm at retail, and FSSAI has explicitly exempted these "traditional" salts from iodization since 2019 ([FSSAI news brief, 16 Jul 2019](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/media/FSSAI_News_Salt_FNB_16_07_2019.pdf)). That matters because iodine is a building block of the thyroid hormones T4 and T3, and the need rises to 250 µg/day in pregnancy and breastfeeding, when deficiency harms fetal brain development ([WHO eLENA](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/commentary/iodine-pregnancy)). India's iodization programme largely works. In 2018-19, women's median urinary iodine was in the adequate range nationally, and goitre in schoolchildren has more than halved since 2003. But about one in four households still lacked adequately iodized salt in 2018-19. No national data after 2021 show coverage falling or deficiency coming back. The "pink salt is bringing deficiency back" story rests on expert warnings and news pieces, not surveys. WHO and ICMR-NIN both say salt reduction and iodization go together: eat less salt, and make that salt iodized. The real iodine risk for women with Hashimoto's is high-dose supplements and kelp pills, not iodized salt in normal cooking amounts. One caveat runs through the whole report: the research team could open very few primary pages because of network blocks, so most claims below are marked as coming from search snippets and need to be checked on the page before a post quotes them.

## How to read the ratings in this report

Each claim carries two labels. **Evidence strength** is Strong (official survey, regulation, WHO guideline or large peer-reviewed study), Moderate (peer-reviewed but small, regional or observational, or an official figure seen only secondhand), or Weak (news, explainer, advocacy, opinion). **Verification** is **[VERIFIED]**, meaning a researcher opened and read the page, or **[SNIPPET]**, meaning the claim came only from search-result text. Only five pages were read in full: Krishna et al. 2022 on iodine losses ([PMC9051721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9051721/)), Žmitek & Pravst 2016 on Slovenian salts ([PMC4963910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4963910/)), Wang et al. 2019 on Zhejiang pregnant women ([PMC6412776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6412776/)), Fayet-Moore 2020 on Australian pink salt ([PMC7603209](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603209)) and Pai 2024 on fluoride in Indian salts ([PMC11380087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11380087)). Everything else, including the WHO, FSSAI, NIH, ATA, NFHS, India Iodine Survey and Lancet sources, is snippet-only. The domains fssai.gov.in, dghs.mohfw.gov.in, ods.od.nih.gov, thyroid.org, nutritionintl.org and thelancet.com were all blocked or returned 403.

## Specialty salts carry almost no iodine unless the pack says iodized

What matters is whether iodine was added. Colour and origin do not decide it. The best data point comes from Slovenia, where 86 products were sampled in 2014. Non-iodized salts, including gourmet sea and Himalayan salt, averaged **3.5 mg KI/kg (range 1.3–5.2)**, while iodized salts averaged **24.2 mg KI/kg**. Iodized sea salt and iodized rock salt both came in around 24 mg/kg, which shows that rock or sea origin is not the point ([Žmitek & Pravst, Nutrients 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4963910/)) [VERIFIED; Strong]. A 2024 New Zealand analysis found non-iodized salts, pink Himalayan among them, at a **median below 2 mg iodine/kg** ([NZMJ 2024](https://nzmj.org.nz/media/pages/journal/vol-137-no-1597/does-iodised-salt-sold-in-new-zealand-contain-enough-iodine/69bd3daa63-1718768420/6475.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Moderate]. Switzerland's food safety office tested 25 salts and called the specialty salts "lacking in iodine", with no advantage over standard salt ([FSVO 2016 report](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/med/staff/cappuccio/who/report_composition-of-prelevant-salt-varieties_2016.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Moderate]. NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements says sea, kosher and Himalayan salt "are not usually iodized" ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)) [SNIPPET; Strong source].

Indian data agree, though the studies are small and the team saw only abstracts. A 2026 University of Delhi study of market salts found black, rock and pink salt had "negligible to undetectable iodine levels". It added that their other minerals are present in such small amounts that eating enough to matter would mean far too much sodium ([Current Research in Nutrition and Food Science, 2026](https://www.foodandnutritionjournal.org/vol14nospl-issue-sustainable-nutrition-2026/from-salt-to-health-iodine-content-in-market-available-salts-and-its-significance/)) [SNIPPET; Moderate, sample size not seen]. Of 14 coloured salts bought in Mohali, only **20%** met the recommended iodine level ([Food Chemistry Advances 2023, via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/640c0728b0b044998aeb90b8a0f2922c)) [SNIPPET; Moderate]. No paper measured kala namak on its own. Do not cite the often-shared Australian pink-salt mineral analysis for iodine, because it never measured iodine ([Fayet-Moore 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603209)) [VERIFIED]. One calculation from these figures, not a published comparison: switching from Indian iodized salt (≥15 ppm at retail) to non-iodized salt (<2–5 ppm) cuts the iodine you get from salt by roughly tenfold or more.

The evidence that people who use these salts end up with less iodine is consistent but observational. In Turkey, 306 pregnant women in their first trimester who used rock salt had a median urinary iodine of **70.9 µg/L**, against **123.7 µg/L** for table-salt users (P < 0.001). The authors also warned that a normal TSH did not reveal the shortfall ([Apaydın et al., Turk J Med Sci 2021](https://journals.tubitak.gov.tr/medical/vol51/iss2/49)) [SNIPPET; Moderate, and the most directly relevant study]. In Zhejiang, among 2,642 pregnant women, non-iodized salt users had a median urinary iodine of 102.2 µg/L against 152.9 µg/L for iodized-salt users inland. Women who believed seafood was the best protection had twice the odds of low iodine ([Wang et al., Nutrients 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6412776/)) [VERIFIED; Moderate-Strong]. That finding is a fair parallel to the Indian belief that "natural" salt covers iodine. No study has measured Indian women who switched to sendha or pink salt. Occasional sendha namak during Navratri fasts is short-term, and no source addresses it.

Iodized salt is not foolproof either. Salt kept in its original pack retained about 92% of its iodine at three months, against about 69% in an open glass jar, and boiling food lost about 40% ([Krishna et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9051721/)) [VERIFIED; Moderate, a review of smaller studies]. That supports practical advice: buy salt within six months of manufacture, keep it in its packet, and keep it away from the stove.

## Thyroid hormone depends on iodine, and pregnancy raises the stakes

Iodine is "an essential component of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)" ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/)) [SNIPPET; Strong, textbook consensus]. WHO and UNICEF raised the pregnancy recommendation from 200 to **250 µg/day** in 2007 and apply the same figure to breastfeeding ([Andersson et al., Public Health Nutrition 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18053281/)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. The reason is that a pregnant woman makes more thyroid hormone, her kidneys clear more iodine and the fetus needs its own supply. Severe deficiency can cause hypothyroidism in both mother and fetus ([WHO eLENA](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/commentary/iodine-pregnancy)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. WHO recommends routine iodine supplements in pregnancy only where fewer than half of households have iodized salt, which does not describe India nationally. The American Thyroid Association's advice of a 150 mcg prenatal supplement is written for the US, where salt iodization is not universal ([ATA statement](https://www.thyroid.org/ata-statement-on-the-potential-risks-of-excess-iodine-ingestion-and-exposure)) [SNIPPET]. Two figures should stay out of a post for now. The "13.5 IQ points" population estimate could not be traced to its primary meta-analysis. The US RDAs of 150/220/290 µg were not confirmed on the page.

For women aged 30 to 50 there is a reassuring baseline. In the India Iodine Survey 2018-19, which covered 21,406 households, the national median urinary iodine was **173.4 µg/L in pregnant women, 172.8 in lactating women and 178.0 in other women**, all within the adequate range ([IIS 2018-19 report](https://www.nutritionintl.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/India-Iodine-Survey-2018-19-final-report.pdf); [J Nutr 2023 record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-37390906)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. National medians hide pockets of deficiency, though. In an eastern Indian hospital study of 70 mothers (Dec 2024–Apr 2025), 18.6% were iodine-deficient before delivery ([IJRCOG](https://www.ijrcog.org/index.php/ijrcog/article/view/16889)) [SNIPPET; Weak-Moderate]. In Uttarakhand, an older study found pregnant women's median iodine of 110–124 µg/L, below the adequacy cut-off ([PubMed 25593836, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25593836/)) [SNIPPET; Moderate]. No Indian dataset reports iodine status for women aged 30 to 50 as a separate group. In short, iodized salt keeps most Indian women adequate, and switching to pink or rock salt removes the one dependable source that keeps them there, which matters most for women planning a pregnancy.

## India's mandate has an exemption and a court challenge

India adopted universal salt iodization in 1983 and runs it through the NIDDCP ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3818611); [DGHS NIDDCP](https://dghs.mohfw.gov.in/niddcp.php)) [SNIPPET; Strong, historical]. FSSAI Regulation 2.3.12 (2011) bars the sale of common salt for direct human consumption unless it is iodized ([FSSAI FAQ, 2021](https://mail.fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/FAQ_Iodised_Salt_28_07_2021.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. The legal minimum is **30 ppm at manufacture and 15 ppm at retail** ([Dept of Consumer Affairs](https://consumeraffairs.nic.in/sites/default/files/file-uploads/ctocpas/Salt.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. The 2019 clarification is the key fact for this post: "traditional salts such as rock salt, black salt, Himalayan pink salt, etc. shall not be mandatorily iodised", and the rule "is meant only for the common salt or white salt" ([FSSAI, 16 Jul 2019](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/media/FSSAI_News_Salt_FNB_16_07_2019.pdf); [Gazette, 4 Jul 2019](https://agriexchange.apeda.gov.in/food_standards/ind_document/Gazette_Notification_Common_Salt_04_07_2019.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Strong, verify on page before quoting].

Do not write that non-iodized salt is banned in India. An Indian Thyroid Society flyer refers to "the recent Madras High Court ruling quashing the mandate for mandatory salt iodisation" ([ITS flyer](https://www.indianthyroidsociety.in/images/ITS%20SALT%20IODISATION%20Flyer.pdf)) [SNIPPET; undated]. An advocacy site dates the ruling to **August 2025**, attributes it to the Madurai Bench on a petition from Tuticorin salt traders, and says the court ordered a new expert committee ([BWC India](https://www.bwcindia.org/web/awareness/learnabout/Salt.html)) [SNIPPET; Weak]. A Deccan Herald op-ed argues the mandate must stand and says the judgment turned on procedure and consumer choice ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/opinion%2Fdon-t-undo-what-we-got-right-why-mandatory-salt-iodisation-must-stand-3712927)) [SNIPPET; opinion]. The ruling's case name, scope, and whether it has been appealed or stayed are all unverified. Rating: Moderate-Weak. Safe wording: "India's iodized-salt rule has been challenged in court."

## Coverage is high but incomplete, and no data show a reversal

India has two headline numbers that measure different things. NFHS-5 (2019-21) found **94.3% of households using iodized salt**, based on a rapid test kit that detects any iodine ([NFHS-5 fact sheet](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/OF43/India_National_Fact_Sheet.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. The India Iodine Survey 2018-19 tested salt for *adequate* iodine (≥15 ppm) and found **76.3%** of households met it. Only 13 of 36 states and UTs reached universal coverage, and Tamil Nadu was lowest ([Nutrition International release](https://nutritionintl.org/news/all-news/nutrition-international-and-partners-disseminate-results-of-india-iodine-survey-2018-19/)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. Loose salt did worst: only **49.7% of crushed and 42.5% of crystal salt samples** were adequately iodized, against about 83% of refined salt ([IIS 2018-19 report](https://www.nutritionintl.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/India-Iodine-Survey-2018-19-final-report.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. So under-iodized salt is a bigger problem than pink salt alone. The DGHS page reportedly says 93.3% of salt samples tested by states in April–December 2024 were adequately iodized ([DGHS NIDDCP](https://dghs.mohfw.gov.in/niddcp.php)) [SNIPPET; Moderate, page unreachable]. That is programme monitoring, not a household survey.

The re-emergence claim is not backed by data. A Newsmeter piece for Global IDD Prevention Day (around October 2025) quotes unnamed experts warning of "a quiet reversal" as urban buyers move to pink, rock and boutique salts ([Newsmeter](https://newsmeter.in/science-health/global-iodine-deficiency-disorders-prevention-day-experts-flag-himalayan-salt-rock-salt-sea-salt-756838)) [SNIPPET; Weak]. A First Check fact-check quotes a dietitian saying cases are "resurfacing" ([First Check](https://firstcheck.in/iodine-deficiency-himalayan-salt-risk/)) [SNIPPET; Weak]. Neither cites survey data. The research found no market data on pink salt sales growth and no national survey after NFHS-5. A 2025 global-burden paper notes that India lacks nationally representative urinary iodine data ([Frontiers in Nutrition 2025](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1577169/pdf)) [SNIPPET]. A post can say experts are worried about the urban switch. It cannot say deficiency is rising nationally. A fitness-club blog claiming a "hypothyroidism crisis" from pink salt cites nothing and should not be used.

## WHO and ICMR say to eat less salt and make it iodized

These two messages do not conflict. WHO recommends adults eat **under 5 g of salt a day** (under 2 g sodium). It also says all salt consumed should be iodized, and that the two policies are compatible because iodine levels can be adjusted as intake falls ([WHO salt reduction fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction); [WHO eLENA, sodium](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/sodium-cvd-adults)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. WHO's 2014 guideline recommends iodizing "all food-grade salt, used in household and food processing" ([WHO 2014](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241507929)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. A 2022 WHO brief calls the two policies "compatible and cost-effective" ([WHO 2022](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240053717)) [SNIPPET; Strong]. WHO's 2025 guideline on potassium-enriched salt substitutes is reported to say these should be iodized too ([Resolve to Save Lives summary](https://resolvetosavelives.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/RTSL_WHO-LSS-Guidelines-Overview.pdf)) [SNIPPET; Moderate, secondary].

India's own guidance says the same. The ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024 say "use iodized salt" and cap added salt at 5 g/day. They also say that "irrespective of the variety of salt, their consumption should be limited as the sodium content in them is almost similar" ([ICMR-NIN DGI 2024](https://www.nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI24thJune2024fin.pdf); [All India Radio](https://newsonair.gov.in/icmr-releases-upgraded-dietary-guidelines-for-indians)) [SNIPPET via news reports; Strong source]. That line undercuts the idea that pink salt is the "low-sodium" choice. Indians eat about **8.0 g/day on average (women 7.1 g)**, according to the ICMR-NCDIR national survey. The survey estimated intake from spot urine samples, which is less accurate than 24-hour collection ([Mathur et al., Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-42694-x)) [SNIPPET; Strong-Moderate].

## The Hashimoto's risk is from supplements, not from iodized salt

The American Thyroid Association advises against iodine or kelp supplements containing **more than 500 mcg/day**. It says intake above 1,100 mcg/day, the tolerable upper limit, "may cause thyroid dysfunction", and it names people with Hashimoto's among those most susceptible to excess iodine. It also finds "no known thyroid benefit" from routine doses above the RDA ([ATA statement](https://www.thyroid.org/ata-statement-on-the-potential-risks-of-excess-iodine-ingestion-and-exposure); [Leung et al., Thyroid 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275241/)) [SNIPPET; Moderate, expert consensus on observational data, and dated 2013/2015]. NIH ODS notes that too much iodine can cause goitre and thyroid inflammation, the same symptoms as too little ([NIH ODS consumer](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-Consumer/)) [SNIPPET; Strong source].

The population evidence is contested. A 2025 systematic review covering 74 studies linked Hashimoto's prevalence partly to excess iodine from salt programmes "without an adequate follow-up and monitoring plan". It also acknowledged that universal iodization has greatly reduced deficiency disorders ([Vargas-Uricoechea et al., Diseases 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40558577/)) [SNIPPET, abstract only; Moderate-Weak]. An earlier review described excess iodine as a trigger for thyroid autoimmunity in some regions ([Luo et al., IJMS 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25050783/)) [SNIPPET; Moderate]. These studies are observational and confounded by more testing, and the effect concentrates where intake overshoots adequacy. Indian women's medians of 172–178 µg/L do not suggest excess. The research found **no guideline telling people with Hashimoto's to avoid iodized salt in normal cooking amounts**. The defensible framing is "official guidance recommends iodized salt for everyone; don't add iodine supplements, kelp or seaweed pills without your doctor". It is not "proven safe for Hashimoto's". Under the repo's health rules, the post should give no iodine doses and say nothing about changing levothyroxine.

## Recent news: one major study, no official releases

The only substantial 2026 item is **ICMR-SALT-T 1** in *The Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia*. It screened 8,903 children aged 10–18 in eight regions between January 2024 and December 2025. Goitre fell from **23.5% (2003) to 10.3%**, and median urinary iodine was 133.2 µg/L, which is sufficient. But 13% had subclinical hypothyroidism and 10.4% had anti-TPO antibodies. The authors conclude these findings "justify continued and strengthened USI implementation" ([Lancet RH-SEA](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lansea/article/PIIS2772-3682(26)00151-4/fulltext); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/health/4-decades-of-salt-iodisation-cut-goitre-in-indian-kids-but-thyroid-autoimmunity-rising-lancet-study/3047879/); [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/goitre-down-thyroid-autoimmunity-up-what-four-decades-of-iodised-salt-show-in-indian-children/)) [SNIPPET, page returned 403; Strong for children only]. One outlet gave a 17% baseline instead of 23.5%. The exact publication date was not visible, so the team could not confirm it falls in October–November 2026. The study is cross-sectional, so it cannot show that iodine caused the antibodies, and it says nothing direct about adult women.

The only coverage of Global IDD Prevention Day (21 October 2026) the team found was on exam-prep sites repeating NFHS-5 and NIDDCP figures ([Adda247](https://currentaffairs.adda247.com/world-iodine-deficiency-day-2026-causes-symptoms-prevention-and-indias-niddcp/); [Usthadian Academy, 24 Oct 2026](https://www.usthadian.com/world-iodine-deficiency-day-2026-and-indias-niddcp/)) [SNIPPET; Weak]. They found no PIB, FSSAI, MoHFW or UNICEF India statement for 2026. The latest official backgrounder found is from October 2024 ([PIB, Oct 2024](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2024/oct/doc20241020419801.pdf)). One unrelated salt story: in February 2026 the Madras High Court ordered microplastics warning labels on plastic-packed salt, and FSSAI withdrew its Supreme Court challenge in May 2026 ([Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/news/litigation/madras-high-court-refuses-to-recall-order-to-disclose-microplastics-in-packaged-water-sugar-salt)) [SNIPPET].

## Conclusion

The pink-salt question in India mostly comes down to labelling and law. The science is settled that these salts carry almost no iodine. What is unusual about India is that its own food regulator lets them sell without iodine and keeps the iodization rule for white salt. A woman who switches her kitchen to sendha or pink salt for "health" is opting out of the safety net, without knowing it, at the life stage when pregnancy planning and thyroid disease make iodine matter most. A more honest and more interesting post than "deficiency is back" would make three points. The national programme works: goitre has halved and women's iodine is adequate. Specialty salts are a legal gap in it. And for women with Hashimoto's, the real risk is the iodine or kelp supplement bottle, not the salt shaker.

Before publishing, someone needs to confirm four things on the actual pages, because none were read in full: the FSSAI 2019 exemption wording, the status of the Madras High Court ruling (via LiveLaw, Bar & Bench or Indian Kanoon), the ICMR-NIN "similar sodium" quote, and the Lancet study's figures and publication date. Two gaps limit any stronger claim: no Indian study has measured iodine in women who switched to pink or rock salt, and no national iodine data exist after 2021. NFHS-6 salt results, when released, will be the first real test of the "quiet reversal" warning.
